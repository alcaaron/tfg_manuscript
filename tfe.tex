During the past few years, the demand on centralized infrastructures has risen considerably. The majority of modern messaging systems rely heavily on cellular networks, internet access, and cloud services. While these infrastructures offer near-global connectivity, they also introduce a Single Point of Failure (SPOF) in the event of natural disasters, blackouts, or network congestion, as well as in remote areas where no coverage is available.

Recent events have exposed the fragility of these infrastructures in emergency scenarios. For instance, a major blackout in Spain on April 18th, 2025, clearly demonstrated this vulnerability. During fires, floods, earthquakes, or massive power outages, cellular networks can be rendered useless or congested, hindering coordination and information exchange. As a consequence, interest in off-grid communication systems, which enable operations without any prior infrastructure dependency, has been significantly boosted.

Mesh networks play a key role in this context because every node can act simultaneously as a transmitter, receiver, and relay, allowing messages to spread between devices via multiple hops. This approach avoids SPOFs and increases tolerance to partial network failures.

Among the wide range of wireless technologies available, LoRa (Long Range) has emerged as an especially suitable option for low-power, long-range communications \cite{dsme_lora_2022}. These characteristics make this technology ideal for off-grid systems, battery-powered devices, and networks deployed in resource-constrained environments.

Furthermore, open-source projects such as Meshtastic reflect growing community interest in LoRa-based off-grid communications \cite{meshtastic}. These systems allow sending text messages and data without the need for cellular coverage or internet access, using low-cost and low-power devices.

This interest extends beyond tech enthusiasts; the telecommunication industry is also exploring hybrid and resilient solutions designed to maintain connectivity even outside traditional coverage. During the 2026 edition of the Mobile World Congress (MWC), companies such as Viasat and Qualcomm presented satellite communication technologies and alternative connectivity systems for vehicles and mobile phones \cite{viasat_mwc_2026}, evidencing the increasing relevance of resilient and off-grid communications.

Nevertheless, despite the technical advantages of these systems, significant accessibility barriers remain for non-technical users. Since the onboarding process of current projects requires advanced configuration knowledge, a primary objective of this thesis is to develop a mesh communication platform that is not only efficient and resilient, but also accessible and intuitive for ordinary users. To achieve this, smartphone integration and a user experience similar to a conventional messaging app will be key characteristics of this project.

Driven by this motivation, this work presents the development of \emph{A3MESH}, a decentralized communication platform based on LoRa and BLE (Bluetooth Low Energy). It is designed to provide resilient, energy-efficient, and easily deployable communication in off-grid or emergency scenarios.

The main objective of this thesis is to design and develop a decentralized low-power mesh communication platform capable of operating without external infrastructure while maintaining scalability, energy efficiency, and accessibility for non-technical users.

To achieve this objective, the project combines hardware design, firmware development, routing optimization, mobile application integration, and real-world validation into a unified communication ecosystem.

The specific objectives of this work are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item Design a portable hardware platform based on LoRa and Bluetooth Low Energy technologies capable of supporting infrastructure-free mesh communication.

\item Develop a modular firmware architecture implementing packet forwarding, neighbor discovery, power management, and communication pipelines between LoRa and BLE interfaces.

\item Investigate the limitations of traditional flooding-based mesh propagation mechanisms and evaluate alternative routing approaches through simulation.

\item Design and implement a routing algorithm capable of dynamically adjusting forwarding behavior according to network conditions.

\item Improve user accessibility by abstracting the underlying networking complexity and providing a user experience similar to conventional messaging applications.

\item Design a compact enclosure and mechanical integration solution suitable for daily portable use alongside smartphones.

\item Validate the proposed system through simulation results, laboratory testing, and real-world communication experiments.
\end{itemize}

The project includes the development of custom hardware, embedded firmware, a routing simulation environment, and a smartphone-oriented mobile application integrated into a complete end-to-end communication system.

The scope of the work covers several engineering areas, including low-power wireless communication, mesh networking algorithms, embedded systems, mobile software development, and mechanical integration.

The implemented system is intended for infrastructure-free communication scenarios such as remote environments, temporary deployments, outdoor activities, and emergency situations where conventional communication infrastructure may be unavailable or unreliable. The proposed platform allows smartphones to exchange messages through nearby mesh nodes using BLE connectivity combined with LoRa-based multi-hop communication.

However, several limitations must also be considered. First, the system has been developed as a prototype-oriented research platform rather than as a commercial product. Consequently, aspects such as industrial certification, large-scale manufacturing optimization, and extensive environmental qualification are outside the scope of this thesis.

Additionally, the mobile application has been developed exclusively for Android devices. Cross-platform compatibility and iOS support were not considered because the primary research focus being centered on mesh networking and embedded communication systems.

The proposed platform also does not rely on Internet gateways, cloud synchronization, or existing communication infrastructure. While this decision improves independence and resilience, it also limits interoperability with traditional messaging services and prevents communication beyond the mesh network itself.

Although the routing algorithm has been evaluated through simulation and validated through real-world testing, large-scale field deployments involving hundreds or thousands of physical nodes were not feasible within the scope of this project. Therefore, scalability conclusions are partially based on simulated environments and controlled experimental conditions.

Finally, while the system incorporates encryption mechanisms and trust-based communication concepts, security-related features are designed to improve practical privacy and usability within the context of low-power decentralized communication systems.

\section{Mesh Communication Systems}
% ~1-1.5 pages
%
% Explain:
% - Mesh networking basics
% - Multi-hop communication
% - Centralized vs distributed systems
% - Advantages/disadvantages
%
% Add:
% - Simple mesh topology figure
Mesh communication systems are decentralized network architectures in which devices, commonly referred to as \emph{nodes}, 
cooperate to exchange information without relying on a fixed centralized infrastructure \cite{mesh_survey,ad_hoc_networks}. Unlike traditional communication 
models based on access points, cellular towers, or centralized servers, mesh networks distribute communication responsibilities 
among all participating nodes, allowing the network to operate autonomously even when part of the infrastructure is unavailable or damaged.

To do so, mesh networks utilize \emph{multi-hop communication}, where messages are forwarded from one node to another until they reach their destination. 
This approach allows for extended coverage beyond the direct communication range \cite{ad_hoc_networks}.

When a node receives a packet that is not intended for itself, it may retransmit the packet to neighboring nodes according to a predefined routing or forwarding strategy. Depending on the network architecture, these strategies 
may rely on deterministic routing tables, probabilistic forwarding mechanisms, flooding approaches, or adaptive routing algorithms.

New nodes can be added dynamically to increase network coverage without requiring significant infrastructure modifications. Additionally, mesh systems can often self-organize and self-heal by adapting communication paths according to network topology changes.

On the one hand, this makes mesh networks particularly suitable for scenarios such as disaster recovery, remote areas, and temporary deployments where traditional 
infrastructure may be unavailable or unreliable. 

On the other hand, one of the most significant problems is routing efficiency. As the number of nodes increases, packet forwarding can generate excessive redundancy, 
channel congestion, collisions, and increased energy consumption. This problem becomes particularly severe in low-power wireless systems where devices are 
battery-powered and communication bandwidth is limited.

Traditional flooding-based approaches represent a simple solution to guarantee high message delivery probability, since every node retransmits received packets.
Nevertheless, this strategy scales poorly in dense networks due to the exponential growth of retransmissions. 
Consequently, modern mesh systems often require more sophisticated routing or forwarding techniques capable of balancing reliability, scalability, and energy efficiency.

Another important limitation is network latency and topology instability. Since communication depends on intermediate nodes, failures or mobility may affect 
message propagation paths and reduce delivery reliability. Furthermore, distributed systems generally require more complex coordination mechanisms 
compared to centralized architectures.

Although centralized communication systems, such as cellular or cloud-based messaging platforms, provide several advantages including global connectivity, 
optimized routing, centralized management, and simplified synchronization, they are fundamentally dependent on infrastructure availability and external service providers.

In contrast, distributed mesh systems sacrifice part of this global connectivity and simplicity in exchange for increased autonomy, resilience, and infrastructure independence.

\subsection{Introduction to LPWAN}

The Internet of Things has created a class of communication requirements that traditional wireless technologies were never designed 
to satisfy: thousands of inexpensive, battery-powered devices, scattered over wide geographical areas, each transmitting only a few 
bytes of data sporadically. Low-Power Wide-Area Networks (LPWANs) emerged precisely to fill this gap, offering kilometre-scale range 
and multi-year battery lifetimes at the cost of very low data rates~\cite{raza2017lpwan}. Short-range technologies such as Wi-Fi, 
Bluetooth and Zigbee provide high throughput but only over tens of metres, while conventional cellular networks offer coverage at the 
expense of complex protocols, subscription costs and energy budgets incompatible with \emph{coin-cell operation}~\cite{mekki2019comparative}. 
LPWAN deliberately accepts the opposite trade-off: it sacrifices throughput in exchange for range and energy efficiency. This makes it 
the natural choice for smart cities, precision agriculture, distributed sensing, remote monitoring and resilient communications in 
infrastructure-degraded scenarios. It is important to stress that LPWAN is not a single technology but a \emph{category} of technologies---including LoRa, Sigfox and NB-IoT---that share these optimisation goals~\cite{raza2017lpwan}.

\subsection{LPWAN versus LoRa: Clarifying the Terminology}

Within this landscape, LoRa (Long Range) is one specific LPWAN technology: a proprietary physical-layer (PHY) modulation developed by 
Semtech~\cite{semtech2015an1200}. A frequent source of confusion is the distinction between LoRa and LoRaWAN. LoRa defines only the radio modulation, 
whereas LoRaWAN is an open MAC and network-layer specification, maintained by the LoRa Alliance, that builds a star-of-stars architecture with gateways and 
network servers on top of the LoRa PHY~\cite{lorawan2017spec}. Crucially, LoRa can be used \emph{without} LoRaWAN: the raw PHY is accessible to any system designer, 
enabling custom protocols above it. This is exactly the approach adopted in A3MESH, which operates directly over the LoRa physical layer to implement a decentralised 
mesh topology rather than the infrastructure-centric model imposed by LoRaWAN. LoRa should therefore be understood as a flexible physical layer on which 
non-conventional architectures can be built.

\subsection{Fundamentals of LoRa}

LoRa is based on Chirp Spread Spectrum (CSS) modulation, in which each symbol is encoded as a \emph{chirp}: a signal whose instantaneous frequency sweeps linearly across 
the entire channel bandwidth~\cite{vangelista2017css}. Unlike narrowband schemes, where all the signal energy is concentrated in a small portion of the spectrum, 
CSS spreads each symbol over the full bandwidth. This spreading yields a substantial processing gain at the receiver, which correlates the incoming signal against 
known chirp patterns and effectively integrates the symbol energy over time. The practical consequences are remarkable: strong robustness against thermal noise and 
narrowband interference, high tolerance to multipath fading and Doppler effects, and---most strikingly---the ability to demodulate signals received 
\emph{below} the noise floor, with sensitivities down to approximately $-137$~dBm~\cite{semtech2015an1200,augustin2016study}. 
The long range of LoRa therefore does not come from transmitting at high power, which is in any case limited by regulation, but from extreme receiver 
sensitivity achieved through spreading and temporal energy integration.

\paragraph{Symbol Representation in CSS.}
At the physical layer, a LoRa symbol is not conveyed by selecting a sinusoidal tone, as in conventional FSK schemes, but by choosing one out of $2^{\mathrm{SF}}$ possible cyclic shifts of a reference chirp. In other words, the information content of the symbol is mapped onto the time-frequency position of the chirp within the symbol interval. From a practical perspective, this can also be interpreted as a discrete frequency offset, where each symbol corresponds to a different starting point along the same linear frequency sweep. As a result, all LoRa symbols occupy the full channel bandwidth and have the same duration and average power; what changes from one symbol to another is only the relative shift of the chirp pattern.

\img{images/chirp_modulation.png}{LoRa symbol-to-chirp mapping~\cite{rfwireless_chirp}.}{0.6}{symbol_chirp}

At the receiver, demodulation is performed by correlating the incoming waveform with a locally generated reference chirp, a process commonly referred to as \emph{dechirping}. This operation converts the cyclic shift of the received chirp into a tone whose frequency is proportional to the transmitted symbol index. The subsequent FFT stage then identifies the spectral bin associated with that tone, allowing the receiver to recover the symbol value efficiently. This is one of the key reasons why LoRa achieves high sensitivity and robust reception under low signal-to-noise ratios~\cite{wirelesspi_lora}.

\subsubsection*{Implications of the Configurable Parameters}
The LoRa PHY therefore exposes a small but highly influential set of parameters that directly shape the link budget, throughput, and robustness of the system. Figure~\ref{fig:lora_modulation} illustrates the effect of the main LoRa parameters on a time-frequency waterfall plot.

\img{images/lora_modulation.png}{LoRa Chirp Spread Spectrum illustration~\cite{wirelesspi_lora}.}{1}{lora_modulation}

% https://wirelesspi.com/understanding-lora-phy-long-range-physical-layer/
% https://www.rfwireless-world.com/terminology/chip-vs-chirp-lorawan#google_vignette
\paragraph{Spreading Factor (SF).} The spreading factor, ranging from SF7 to SF12, determines the number of distinct \emph{cyclic shifts} that can be applied to a chirp, yielding up to $2^{\mathrm{SF}}$ unique symbols. 
By definition, the symbol duration is $T_s = 2^{\mathrm{SF}}/BW$~\cite{semtech2015an1200}. Each unit increase in SF roughly doubles the airtime, so its effect on time-on-air, 
energy consumption and latency is exponential, while sensitivity improves. Higher SFs thus extend range at the cost of drastically 
reduced bitrate. Different SFs are quasi-orthogonal, allowing partially concurrent transmissions, although this orthogonality is imperfect in 
practice~\cite{croce2018orthogonality}. Besides, in a mesh context this trade-off is critical: high SFs improve connectivity between distant nodes but inflate 
channel occupancy, increasing congestion and the probability of collisions as the network grows~\cite{bor2016scale}.

\paragraph{Bandwidth (BW).} LoRa typically operates with bandwidths of 125, 250 or 500~kHz. Narrower bandwidths capture less thermal noise and therefore improve 
sensitivity, but they proportionally lengthen symbols and reduce data rate~\cite{augustin2016study}. As with SF, the designer trades speed for robustness.

\paragraph{Coding Rate (CR).} LoRa incorporates Forward Error Correction with coding rates of 4/5, 4/6, 4/7 and 4/8. Higher redundancy improves the 
receiver's ability to recover corrupted symbols in noisy or interference-prone environments, at the cost of lower effective throughput and longer 
airtime, i.e.\ reduced spectral efficiency~\cite{semtech2015an1200}.

\paragraph{Other parameters.} Transmission power (typically up to +20~dBm in the European 868~MHz ISM band) directly shapes the link budget, range and energy consumption. 
The preamble length controls how reliably receivers detect and synchronise to incoming frames, an important lever for duty-cycled listeners. 
An optional CRC protects payload integrity, and the sync word provides logical separation between co-located networks sharing the same channel~\cite{semtech2015an1200}.

\subsection{Fundamental Trade-offs}

No LoRa configuration simultaneously maximises range, throughput, latency and spectral efficiency. The two extremes of the configuration space illustrate this 
clearly: SF12 with 125~kHz bandwidth and CR~4/8 yields maximum range and robustness, with airtimes of over a second for modest payloads, whereas SF7 
with 500~kHz and CR~4/5 maximises throughput and minimises latency but sharply reduces link margin. Airtime is the variable that couples these choices to 
system-level behaviour: longer frames raise collision probability, degrade scalability and consume the limited 
duty-cycle budget (typically 1\% in the EU 868~MHz sub-bands under ETSI regulations)~\cite{adelantado2017limits,bor2016scale}.

\subsection{Practical Limitations of LoRa}

A mature assessment must acknowledge LoRa's limitations. Bitrates range only from roughly 0.3 to 27~kbps, and transceivers are half-duplex, 
unable to receive while transmitting. High airtimes at large SFs amplify the classical hidden-node problem, since carrier sensing is unreliable over 
kilometre-scale links, and collisions grow rapidly with network density~\cite{bor2016scale,adelantado2017limits}. 
Regulatory duty-cycle restrictions in ISM bands further cap achievable throughput and limit how often nodes may relay traffic. 
Finally, LoRa was conceived for star topologies, not mesh networking: it provides no native routing, scheduling or neighbour-discovery mechanisms. 
These shortcomings do not invalidate the technology, but they imply that an efficient mesh network over LoRa requires additional protocol 
mechanisms at the MAC and routing layers.

\subsection{Why LoRa}

After evaluating alternative technologies, all fail at least one requirement: Wi-Fi and BLE lack the necessary range; Zigbee meshes well but only over short links; 
NB-IoT depends entirely on operator-owned cellular infrastructure, an unacceptable single point of failure in disaster scenarios; and Sigfox relies on a proprietary, 
operator-managed network with severely constrained uplink and almost no downlink capability~\cite{mekki2019comparative}. 
LoRa, in contrast, operates in licence-free ISM bands without any operator, offers kilometre-scale links at milliwatt power levels, and exposes a highly configurable 
PHY that permits experimentation with custom mesh protocols. For these reasons---autonomy, range, energy efficiency, regulatory accessibility and PHY flexibility---LoRa 
was selected as the physical layer of this project, with the understanding that the mesh-specific challenges identified above must be solved by the protocol design presented in 
the following chapters.

\section{Bluetooth Low Energy}
\label{sec:ble}

Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), introduced in version 4.0 of the Bluetooth Core
Specification, is a short-range wireless technology designed from the ground
up for ultra-low-power operation rather than as a mere optimisation of classic
Bluetooth~\cite{bluetooth2023core,gomez2012overview}. Although it shares the
brand and the 2.4~GHz ISM band with its predecessor, BLE is a distinct
protocol stack: it uses 40 channels of 2~MHz with GFSK modulation, three of
which are reserved for advertising, and it replaces the connection-oriented
complexity of classic Bluetooth with a lightweight, asynchronous design
oriented towards the sporadic exchange of small amounts of data.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Architecture}

The BLE stack is organised into three blocks. The \emph{controller} comprises
the physical layer and the link layer, which manages device states
(advertising, scanning, initiating, and connected), adaptive frequency hopping,
and connection events. The \emph{host} contains the Logical Link Control and
Adaptation Protocol (L2CAP), the Attribute Protocol (ATT), the Generic
Attribute Profile (GATT), the Security Manager, and the Generic Access Profile
(GAP), which defines discovery and connection roles such as \emph{central} and
\emph{peripheral}. Both blocks communicate through the Host Controller
Interface (HCI)~\cite{bluetooth2023core,gomez2012overview}.

Communication follows two complementary patterns: connectionless broadcasting
over the advertising channels, and point-to-point connections in which the
central device polls the peripheral at a negotiated \emph{connection interval},
allowing the radio to remain dormant between events.

% ---- GAP operations ------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Advertising and Scanning}

To establish a connection, one device must advertise while a second device
scans. The advertiser periodically transmits advertising packets on the three
dedicated channels; the scanner listens and raises a scan-report event each
time such a packet is received~\cite{silabs_ble_flowcharts}. Two scanning
modes are available: in \emph{passive} scanning the scanner only receives
advertising packets, whereas in \emph{active} scanning it additionally
transmits a scan-request frame containing its own Bluetooth address, allowing
the advertiser to respond with supplementary data. BLE implements a
time-division duplex scheme, so all timing is governed by three tuneable
parameters---advertising interval, scanning interval, and scanning
window---whose interaction determines discovery latency and energy
consumption~\cite{bluetooth2023core}.

\subsubsection{Connection Establishment}

Once the scanner has collected sufficient information from the advertiser, it
may open a connection, at which point it becomes the \emph{central} device and
the former advertiser becomes the \emph{peripheral}~\cite{silabs_ble_flowcharts}.
A connection-initiation failure can occur in two ways: the central explicitly
cancels the pending request, or the procedure times out because the peripheral
does not respond within the prescribed interval. The BLE standard defines a
hard limit of six consecutive connection intervals during which either host may
remain silent; if this threshold is exceeded, the link layer drops the
connection~\cite{bluetooth2023core,silabs_ble_flowcharts}.

\subsubsection{Connection Parameter Negotiation}

After a connection has been established, both hosts may request adjustments to
the link parameters. The connection-parameter-update procedure can be triggered
either by the central, the peripheral, or autonomously by the link layer during
connection initiation. In all cases the central retains the final authority over
parameter selection~\cite{silabs_ble_flowcharts}. A related sub-procedure, the
\emph{GATT MTU exchange}, is also performed at this stage: the ATT protocol
defines a default Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU) of 23~bytes, but if both
devices support a larger value the MTU exchange procedure negotiates a higher
limit, executed exactly once per connection~\cite{bluetooth2023core}.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Generic Attribute Profile (GATT)}

Data exchange in connected mode is structured by GATT, a client--server model
built on top of ATT. A GATT server exposes its state as a hierarchy of
\emph{services}, each grouping one or more \emph{characteristics}: typed
attributes identified by UUIDs that a client can read, write, or subscribe to
through \emph{notifications} and
\emph{indications}~\cite{bluetooth2023core}. This self-describing structure
permits a client to discover at runtime what data a previously unknown device
offers, without any out-of-band agreement. In practice, GATT acts as a
universal application-layer contract: any BLE-capable device, including every
modern smartphone, can interact with a custom peripheral by knowing only its
service and characteristic UUIDs.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Low-Power Operation}

BLE achieves multi-year operation on coin-cell batteries through aggressive
duty cycling rather than reduced transmit power alone. Connection events and
advertising bursts last only a few milliseconds, after which the radio returns
to deep sleep; protocol overhead is minimal, and device discovery and
connection establishment complete in milliseconds rather than
seconds~\cite{gomez2012overview,tosi2017performance}. Parameters such as the
connection interval, slave latency, and advertising interval allow the designer
to trade latency for energy. Reported peak currents in the range of tens of
milliamperes, combined with sleep currents of a few microamperes, yield average
power consumptions that are orders of magnitude below those of Wi-Fi or classic
Bluetooth for intermittent traffic~\cite{siekkinen2012energy}.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Suitability for Smartphone Integration}

BLE's native support in all major mobile operating systems---iOS, Android, and
their derivatives---makes it the standard for peripheral-to-smartphone
communication. The GATT model abstracts hardware specifics behind a uniform
service interface, so a single mobile application can interact with any
compliant peripheral without platform-specific drivers. Combined with the
sub-second connection times and the minimal radio overhead imposed on the
handset, BLE provides the connectivity layer that enables the local
configuration and monitoring interface examined in the remainder of this
work.

\subsection{BLE as the Smartphone Bridge}

The decisive argument for BLE in this project is ubiquity: native BLE support is mandatory hardware in virtually all smartphones manufactured in the last 
decade, and both Android and iOS expose mature GATT APIs to applications, with no need for pairing infrastructure, routers or licensed 
spectrum~\cite{gomez2012overview}. In A3MESH, BLE therefore plays a role complementary to LoRa: while LoRa provides the long-range 
backbone between nodes, BLE provides the last metre between each node and its user. The node acts as a GATT peripheral exposing message characteristics, and the smartphone application, acting as central, becomes the human interface of the mesh without requiring any 
custom radio hardware on the user's side. The combination of negligible energy cost, standardised application-layer semantics through GATT, 
and universal smartphone availability makes BLE the natural choice for this local link.

\section{Existing Solutions}
\label{sec:existing-solutions}

Building multi-hop networks over LoRa is an active area of both academic research and community-driven development. Surveys of the field show that, although LoRaWAN's star-of-stars topology remains dominant, a growing ecosystem of projects explores mesh architectures over the raw LoRa PHY~\cite{pueyo2021beyond,wong2024multihop}. Among them, two open-source systems are particularly relevant to this thesis because they target the same use case and because they embody two opposite points in the routing design space: Meshtastic, based on network-wide flooding, and MeshCore (deployed at scale by initiatives such as LocalMesh), based on structured routing through dedicated repeaters.

\subsection{Meshtastic}
\label{subsec:meshtastic}
% Explain:
% - Architecture
% - Flooding strategy
% - Pros/cons
% - What your system improves

Meshtastic is an open-source firmware and application ecosystem that turns inexpensive ESP32-based LoRa boards into an off-grid messaging platform~\cite{meshtastic2024docs}. Its architecture is deliberately egalitarian: every node runs the same stack, pairs with a smartphone over BLE for the user interface, and---by default---participates in packet forwarding. There is no central infrastructure, no network server and no special node required for the network to function, which makes deployment trivially simple.

Routing in Meshtastic follows a \emph{managed flooding} strategy. A node that originates a message broadcasts it; every node that receives it rebroadcasts it once, until a hop-limit counter (three by default, seven at most) is exhausted~\cite{meshtastic2024mesh}. The flooding is ``managed'' through a handful of heuristics: duplicate packets are recognised and suppressed via a per-packet identifier cache; rebroadcasts are delayed by a contention window weighted by the received SNR, so that distant nodes---which extend coverage the most---tend to retransmit first; and a node that overhears another copy of the packet during its back-off cancels its own retransmission. Since version 2.6, unicast messages additionally learn a next-hop after the first flooded delivery, although broadcast traffic, which dominates typical usage, continues to flood~\cite{meshtastic2024mesh}.

The strengths of this design are clear. Flooding requires no routing state, no topology knowledge and no control traffic; it is maximally robust to mobility and node failure, since any available path is implicitly used; and it guarantees that broadcasts reach every reachable node. These properties explain Meshtastic's popularity for hiking groups, events and emergency-preparedness communities. The weaknesses, however, are equally structural. Every message triggers up to one transmission per node within the hop horizon, so channel occupancy grows with node density rather than with traffic demand---a particularly serious problem over LoRa, where airtime is long and duty-cycle budgets are tight. Experimental and community evidence consistently reports degraded delivery rates and channel saturation as dense meshes grow towards the order of a hundred nodes, aggravated by periodic telemetry and position broadcasts that the protocol itself generates~\cite{wong2024multihop,meshtastic2024mesh}. In other words, Meshtastic inherits the classical \emph{broadcast storm} pathology discussed in Section~\ref{sec:gossip}, mitigated but not solved by its heuristics.

A3MESH takes Meshtastic's architectural premises---full decentralisation, role-free nodes, BLE smartphone integration---as a baseline, but replaces blind rebroadcasting with a probabilistic, adaptive forwarding discipline. The goal is to retain flooding's robustness and statelessness while decoupling channel load from node density, so that the network degrades gracefully precisely in the dense, high-stress scenarios where flooding performs worst.

\subsection{LocalMesh and MeshCore}
\label{subsec:localmesh}

LocalMesh is a community initiative, originating in the Netherlands and the United Kingdom, that deploys volunteer-operated LoRa repeaters to provide free, subscription-less emergency messaging when cellular and Internet infrastructure fail~\cite{localmesh2026}. Technically, LocalMesh networks run MeshCore, an open-source mesh protocol released in 2025 as a direct response to the scalability limits of flood-based systems~\cite{meshcore2025}.

MeshCore abandons the egalitarian model in favour of explicit device roles. Ordinary user devices (\emph{companions}) never forward traffic; forwarding is reserved for dedicated \emph{repeaters}, intentionally placed at elevated locations, while \emph{room servers} provide store-and-forward group messaging. Routing is hybrid: the first message towards an unknown destination is flooded through the repeater backbone for path discovery; the acknowledgement returns the ordered sequence of repeaters traversed, and subsequent messages embed this source-routed path in their headers, so that only the designated next-hop repeater forwards each packet. If a cached path fails, the sender falls back to flooding to discover a fresh one~\cite{meshcore2025}. The result is a much quieter channel: after path discovery, most traffic is directed unicast rather than network-wide broadcast, which is why MeshCore networks scale comfortably beyond the practical limits of Meshtastic.

These gains, however, are bought with assumptions that cannot be made in this project. MeshCore's efficiency depends on a \emph{planned, static backbone} of well-placed, always-on repeaters---volunteer infrastructure that takes months to build and that constitutes, in aggregate, a dependency not unlike the one resilient networks try to escape. Cached source routes also presume topological stability: under mobility or node loss, the protocol repeatedly falls back to flooding, eroding its advantage exactly when conditions are most adverse. A3MESH targets the complementary scenario: spontaneous, fully ad-hoc deployments of homogeneous, possibly mobile nodes, with no pre-positioned infrastructure and no role assignment. The research question is therefore whether the channel-load discipline that MeshCore achieves through \emph{structure} can instead be achieved through \emph{statistics}---that is, through gossip-based forwarding.

\subsection{The Flooding Problem}

Flooding is the simplest possible dissemination primitive: every node retransmits every new packet exactly once. Its appeal in ad-hoc networks is that it requires no routes, no neighbour tables and no coordination, and it finds every reachable node by construction. Its cost was characterised in the seminal analysis of the \emph{broadcast storm problem} by Ni et al., who showed that naive rebroadcasting produces severe redundancy, contention and collisions: in dense topologies, each additional rebroadcast covers only a marginal sliver of new area while every transmission competes for the shared channel~\cite{ni1999broadcast}. The problem is dramatically amplified over LoRa, where frames occupy the channel for hundreds of milliseconds at high spreading factors, transceivers are half-duplex, carrier sensing is unreliable over kilometre-scale links (the hidden-node problem), and regulatory duty cycles cap each node's airtime budget~\cite{adelantado2017limits,bor2016scale}. A LoRa mesh that floods therefore saturates at modest node counts, as the Meshtastic experience illustrates.

\subsection{Probabilistic Forwarding}

Gossip protocols attack this redundancy at its root by randomising the forwarding decision: upon first reception of a packet, a node rebroadcasts it with probability $p$ and stays silent with probability $1-p$. The foundational result of Haas, Halpern and Li is that this simple mechanism exhibits a \emph{bistable} (percolation-like) behaviour: below a critical probability the dissemination dies out quickly, while above it the message reaches almost all nodes almost as reliably as full flooding. In typical random topologies the threshold lies around $p \approx 0.6$--$0.8$, meaning that 25--35\% of transmissions can simply be removed with negligible loss of coverage~\cite{haas2006gossip}. For LoRa meshes this is a directly monetisable saving: fewer transmissions translate into proportionally less airtime, fewer collisions, lower energy consumption and slower duty-cycle exhaustion.

\subsection{Controlled Gossip}

Pure probabilistic forwarding has a known weakness: dissemination can die prematurely in sparse regions, where a node may be the \emph{only} bridge between two parts of the network yet still decline to forward. A family of \emph{controlled} gossip variants addresses this by combining the coin flip with deterministic safeguards~\cite{haas2006gossip,williams2002comparison}. Representative mechanisms include forwarding with probability 1 for the first $k$ hops to give the message critical mass (GOSSIP1$(p,k)$); forcing retransmission when a node has fewer neighbours than a threshold, so that articulation points never stay silent; and counter-based recovery, in which a node that has not overheard sufficient copies of a packet within a timeout concludes that the gossip is dying in its vicinity and retransmits regardless of its earlier decision. These hybrids retain most of the savings of pure gossip while restoring flooding-like reliability in irregular topologies---an essential property for A3MESH, whose deployments are expected to mix dense clusters with sparse long-range bridges.

\subsection{Adaptive Gossip and the Research Gap}

The protocols above still share a limitation: $p$ and its companion thresholds are fixed at design time, while the optimal operating point depends on local density, traffic load and link quality, all of which vary across the network and over time. \emph{Adaptive} gossip closes this loop by letting each node tune its forwarding probability from locally observable signals. Kyasanur et al.'s Smart Gossip, for instance, infers parent--child dependencies from overheard traffic and assigns each node a forwarding probability proportional to how much its neighbours actually depend on it, converging automatically to high probabilities in sparse regions and low ones in dense regions, with no manual configuration~\cite{kyasanur2006smartgossip}. Related adaptive schemes modulate $p$ using neighbour counts, overheard duplicate counters or received signal strength~\cite{williams2002comparison}.

This body of work, however, was developed and evaluated almost exclusively for IEEE~802.11 ad-hoc networks and classical wireless sensor networks---radios with millisecond airtimes, effective carrier sensing and no duty-cycle constraints. Its translation to LoRa is far from mechanical: long airtimes magnify the cost of every redundant transmission, half-duplex operation means a forwarding node is deaf while it helps others, SNR is cheaply observable and unusually informative thanks to CSS, and the duty cycle imposes a hard budget. Meanwhile, the deployed LoRa mesh systems reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:existing-solutions} occupy the two extremes of the spectrum---managed flooding without probabilistic suppression (Meshtastic) and structured routing requiring planned infrastructure (MeshCore)---leaving the middle ground essentially unexplored. This is the research gap addressed by this thesis: A3MESH brings \emph{smart, adaptive gossip} to a fully decentralised LoRa mesh, using locally available information such as RSSI and overheard duplicates to modulate each node's forwarding probability, with the aim of approaching the channel efficiency of structured routing while preserving the zero-infrastructure, zero-configuration resilience of flooding.

\section{Node Interaction Model}
\label{sec:node_interaction_model}
% Explain:
% - Neighbor interaction
% - Discovery
% - Message forwarding
% - Broadcast behavior
The mesh layer is built on a deliberately egalitarian premise: all nodes are functionally identical peers. There are no coordinators, no dedicated repeaters and no infrastructure roles of any kind;
every node is simultaneously an end point for its own user and a potential relay for the traffic of others.
This design choice follow directly from the resilience objectives of the system and stands in explicit contrast to the role-based architecture of MeshCore discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:localmesh}.

\paragraph{Neighbor awareness.} Each node maintains a local notion of its \emph{neighborhood}: the set of nodes whose transmissions it can hear directly. Crucially, this awareness is acquired \emph{passively}. Since every LoRa transmission is inherently a broadcast, a node learns of the existence, identity and link quality of its neighbors simply by overhearing the traffic that already flows through the channel.

\paragraph{Message propagation.} Communication beyond direct radio range is achieved through multi-hop forwarding. Indeed, the logical level distinguishes between a direct message and a network-wide announcement, encoded in the packet's addressing fields. With regard to the latter a node receiving a packet that is not addressed to it does not discard it, but evaluates whether to act as a relay.

\paragraph{Forwarding decision.} The defining characteristic of A3MESH's interaction model is that this relaying decision is \emph{probabilistic and adaptive} rather than deterministic. Instead of rebroadcasting every new packet unconditionally---the flooding approach whose pathologies were analysed in Section~\ref{sec:gossip}---each node forwards with a probability modulated by locally observable signals, such as the quality of the link over which the packet arrived and the level of redundancy it observes in its vicinity. For instance: nodes in dense regions, where many candidate relays exist, suppress most of their retransmissions, whereas nodes in sparse regions, where they may constitute the only bridge between network segments, forward with near certainty. As a result the network as a whole thereby self-regulates its channel occupancy without any global coordination. Moreover, the design of this forwarding policy, its mathematical formulation and its optimisation constitute the central contribution of this thesis and are developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation} together with its realisation in the firmware-dedicated Chapter~\ref{ch:firmware}.

\paragraph{Origination.} The lifecycle begins in the mobile application, where the user composes a message addressed to a contact. The application encodes the request as an AT command and writes it to the corresponding GATT characteristic of its paired node over BLE. From the user's perspective the interaction ends here: the experience is intentionally indistinguishable from that of a conventional messaging application.

\paragraph{Ingestion at the originating node.} The node's firmware parses the AT command, validates it, and constructs a mesh packet: the payload is encrypted, the addressing fields, sequence number and time-to-live (TTL) counter are populated, and the resulting frame is serialised for transmission. The node then transmits the packet over the LoRa radio as a broadcast, subject to the channel-access and duty-cycle constraints enforced by the firmware.

\paragraph{Mesh traversal.} Every node within radio range receives the frame. Each receiver first checks the packet against its duplicate cache; previously seen packets are silently discarded, which guarantees that no packet is relayed more than once by the same node. For a new packet, the node performs two independent actions. First, it checks whether the packet is addressed to itself, in which case it is passed up to the local delivery path. Second---and regardless of addressing---it evaluates the adaptive forwarding decision described in Section~\ref{sec:node_interaction_model}: with the computed probability, it schedules a rebroadcast after a randomised back-off that desynchronises concurrent relays; otherwise it remains silent. Each rebroadcast decrements the TTL, which bounds the propagation horizon and prevents indefinite circulation. The packet thus expands through the network in stochastic waves, hop by hop, until it reaches the destination, the TTL expires, or the gossip extinguishes naturally once coverage is achieved.

\paragraph{Delivery.} When the packet reaches a node whose identity matches the destination address, the firmware decrypts and validates the payload, stores the message, and notifies the paired smartphone through a BLE notification on the corresponding GATT characteristic. The recipient's application then decodes the notification and renders the message in the conversation view. % If the destination smartphone is momentarily disconnected, the node retains the message and delivers it upon reconnection, so that the user's handset need not be permanently linked for the system to operate.

It must be emphasised that this description captures the system-level journey of a message; the internal mechanics of each stage---the queueing and task structure of the BLE-to-LoRa pipelines, the channel-access procedure, and the encryption scheme---are implementation concerns addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:firmware}.


\section{Design Requirements}
\label{sec:design_requirements}
The architecture described above is shaped by four system-level requirements. They are stated here together with their rationale and target figures; the subsequent chapters derive from them the concrete engineering requirements of each subsystem (Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware} for the hardware platform, Chapter~\ref{ch:firmware} for the firmware, and Chapter~\ref{ch:mobileapp} for the mobile application), and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} evaluates the extent to which they are met.

\subsection{Low Power Consumption}
\label{subsec:req_power}
% Explain power constraints and goals
A3MESH nodes are battery-powered devices intended for scenarios---emergencies, remote deployments, prolonged infrastructure outages---in which recharging opportunities cannot be assumed. Energy is therefore the most binding resource of the entire design, and the requirement is twofold. At the device level, a node must sustain multiple days of autonomous operation on a single battery charge, which demands aggressive duty cycling of both radios, exploitation of the ESP32 sleep modes and a power subsystem with high conversion efficiency. At the network level, the forwarding protocol itself must be energy-aware: every retransmission suppressed by the gossip policy is energy saved not by one node but by the network as a whole. This dual perspective---energy as a hardware constraint and as a protocol design criterion---recurs throughout this work.

\subsection{Scalability}
\label{subsec:req_scalability}
% Explain node density concerns
The system must remain functional as node density grows, precisely the regime in which flood-based LoRa meshes degrade. The requirement is therefore formulated in terms of \emph{graceful degradation}: channel load must grow with offered traffic rather than with the number of deployed nodes, and the addition of nodes to a region should improve, not erode, its connectivity. This requirement is the principal motivation for abandoning deterministic flooding in favour of the adaptive gossip policy, and it is evaluated quantitatively through the congestion metrics of Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation}.

\subsection{Reliability}
\label{subsec:req_reliability}
% Explain delivery reliability goals
A messaging system intended for emergency use must deliver messages with high probability under adverse conditions: lossy links, node failures, mobility and network partitions. Probabilistic forwarding introduces an inherent tension here, since every suppressed retransmission is also a lost redundancy opportunity. The requirement is therefore expressed as a target operating point: the forwarding policy must achieve a packet delivery ratio statistically indistinguishable from that of pure flooding---in particular in sparse topologies, where premature gossip die-out is the dominant failure mode---while consuming a substantially smaller airtime budget. Reliability is thus not pursued through end-to-end acknowledgements and retransmissions, which would multiply airtime consumption, but through the controlled redundancy that the gossip mechanism itself provides. The delivery-ratio metric defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation} quantifies compliance with this requirement.

\subsection{User Accessibility}
\label{subsec:req_accessibility}
% Explain ease-of-use philosophy
Finally, the system must be operable by users with no technical background whatsoever. In the intended scenarios, the people who most need resilient communication are not radio amateurs or engineers, and any configuration step---flashing firmware, selecting spreading factors, managing identifiers---constitutes a barrier that excludes them. This requirement drives the upper layers of the architecture: the smartphone, a device users already own and understand, is the sole human interface; the BLE link and pairing process require no networking knowledge; the mesh is self-configuring, with discovery, forwarding and adaptation occurring autonomously; and the application deliberately reproduces the interaction patterns of mainstream messaging applications. Accessibility also constrains the physical design, as reflected in the ergonomic and enclosure decisions of the mechanical integration chapter. In short, all technical complexity of the system is required to remain invisible below the application layer.

\section{Purpose of the Simulator}
% Explain:
% - Why simulation was necessary
% - Why real testing alone is insufficient
% - Parameter exploration problem
There are several reasons why a network simulator was necessary for this thesis.

The first is the \emph{parameter exploration problem}. The adaptive forwarding function developed in this chapter is governed by five continuous parameters, where each evaluation of a single parameter combination requires observing the network for a statistically meaningful period. In other words, performing even a coarse exploration of this space on real hardware would require thousands of multi-hour field deployments, which is materially impossible within the scope of this work. In contrast, in simulation the same exploration is reduced to minutes of computation.

The second obstacle is \emph{scale and controllability}. The phenomena of interest---broadcast storms, channel congestion, gossip die-out---only emerge at network sizes of tens of nodes spread over kilometres, far beyond the prototype fleet that can realistically be manufactured and deployed. Moreover, physical experiments cannot hold conditions constant: interference, weather and node placement vary between runs, making it impossible to attribute performance differences to the routing algorithm rather than to the environment. 

Finally, a discrete-event simulator provides perfectly repeatable conditions in which two algorithms can be compared on \emph{identical} topologies, traffic patterns and channel realisations.

\subsection{Simulator Architecture}
The experimental platform is built upon \emph{Meshtasticator}, the open-source discrete-event simulator of the Meshtastic project~\cite{meshtasticator2024}. Meshtasticator models a LoRa mesh as a set of node processes coordinated by the SimPy discrete-event engine~\cite{simpy2024}: nodes exchange packets through a broadcast pipe that emulates the shared radio medium, and every transmission is propagated to all other nodes, where it may be received, lost due to insufficient signal strength, or destroyed by collision. The simulator implements the relevant PHY and MAC behaviour of real Meshtastic devices as well as LoRa time-on-air computation, capture-effect collision modelling, listen-before-talk channel access with random back-off, and the official \emph{managed flood} routing algorithm of the Meshtastic firmware, which provides a faithful baseline for comparison.

On top of that, the simulator was extended for this thesis in two main directions. First, the routing layer was refactored to support pluggable forwarding policies, and the candidate strategies described in Section~\ref{sec:sim_strategies} were implemented as selectable router types, culminating in the Smart Gossip router. Second, the configuration system was instrumented so that the parameters of the Smart Gossip decision function are exposed as runtime variables, a prerequisite for the automated optimisation process of Section~\ref{sec:sim_optimization}. Finally, a batch-execution harness and an optimisation driver were developed on top of the simulator to run large experiment campaigns and collect the metrics defined in Section~\ref{sec:sim_metrics}.

\subsection{Node, Packet and Channel Models}
Each simulated node generates application messages, maintains a duplicate cache, executes the configured forwarding policy and respects the half-duplex constraint of the radio. Nodes are placed uniformly at random over a 15$\times$15~km area, subject to a minimum-separation constraint, and approximately 30\% of the nodes are mobile, exercising the protocols under topological change.

A part from this, the radio configuration corresponds to the \texttt{LONG\_FAST} preset on the EU~868 band: SF11, 250~kHz bandwidth and coding rate 4/5, yielding a time-on-air of approximately 550~ms per packet; transmit power is 27~dBm and receiver sensitivity $-131.5$~dBm. Path loss follows the 3GPP Suburban Macro model (Model~5), which produces realistic link ranges of roughly 1.8~km in this configuration. Two refinements increase realism: log-normal shadowing and per-link asymmetric gain offsets (normally distributed, $\sigma = 3$~dB), so that link quality differs between directions, as observed in real LoRa deployments~\cite{cattani2017reliability}. The hop limit is fixed at 3, matching the Meshtastic default.


\subsection{Randomness and Reproducibility}
 
Every stochastic element of the simulation---node placement, traffic generation, link asymmetries, mobility and the gossip coin flips themselves---derives from explicitly seeded pseudo-random generators. Each repetition of a scenario uses a distinct, documented seed (0--29), and node positions are pre-generated and cached per seed, so that all routing algorithms are evaluated on \emph{exactly} the same sequence of topologies. What is more, this pairing eliminates topology variance from inter-algorithm comparisons and makes every result in this chapter exactly reproducible from the configuration files listed in Appendix~E.

\section{Evaluated Routing Strategies}
\label{sec:sim_strategies}

The design of the final algorithm proceeded incrementally: a sequence of forwarding strategies of increasing sophistication was implemented and evaluated, each addressing a weakness identified in its predecessor. This section describes the progression conceptually; quantitative results are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:sim_results}.

\subsection{Pure Flooding}
% Explain:
% - Operation
% - Advantages
% - Massive redundancy problem
Pure flooding is the natural baseline: every node retransmits every packet it has not seen before, until the hop limit is exhausted. Its virtues are simplicity and maximal coverage---if any path exists within the hop horizon, flooding finds it. Its pathology, anticipated analytically in Section~\ref{sec:gossip}, was confirmed immediately in simulation: the number of transmissions grows linearly with node count regardless of traffic demand, and with LoRa airtimes of half a second per packet the channel saturates rapidly, manifesting as collisions, queueing delays and duty-cycle exhaustion. Meshtastic's managed flood, which adds SNR-weighted contention windows and rebroadcast cancellation, mitigates the worst simultaneity but, as the results of Section~\ref{sec:sim_results} show, does not alter the fundamental scaling behaviour. Managed flood, being the algorithm deployed in the most widely used comparable system, serves as the reference baseline throughout this chapter.

\subsection{Probabilistic Gossip}
% Explain:
% - Random forwarding probability
% - Trade-offs
The first refinement replaces the deterministic rebroadcast with a Bernoulli trial: each node forwards a new packet with fixed probability $p$ and stays silent otherwise, following the classical gossip scheme of Haas et al.~\cite{haas2006gossip}. After simulation, several values of $p$ were tested, and the scheme's weakness is equally clear: no single value of $p$ suits all regions of a heterogeneous network. Therefore, a probability low enough to relieve congestion in dense clusters causes premature die-out in sparse areas, where a silent node may be the only bridge between partitions; a probability high enough to protect sparse areas forfeits most of the savings in dense ones. Consequently, the optimal $p$ is, in short, a \emph{local} quantity---which motivates making the decision adaptive.

This routing logic is defined as \emph{GOSSIP0N} in the simulator, where N value is represented between 2 and 8, related to the values of $p$ tested.
\subsection{RSSI-Aware Gossip}
% Explain:
% - Link quality usage
The second refinement modulates the forwarding probability with the received signal strength of the incoming packet. The intuition exploits a geometric fact of broadcast propagation: a packet received weakly arrives from far away, meaning the receiver lies near the edge of the transmitter's coverage area and is precisely the kind of node that extends the packet's reach furthest if it relays; conversely, a packet received strongly comes from a nearby transmitter whose coverage area largely overlaps the receiver's, making a relay largely redundant. RSSI thus acts as a zero-cost proxy for geographic progress, an idea well established in WSN and LPWAN routing~\cite{senel2011relay,cattani2017reliability}. This strategy concentrates retransmissions at the periphery of each broadcast wave, improving the usefulness of each transmission, but it remains blind to how many \emph{other} candidates surround the receiver.

This routing logic is defined as \emph{RSSI\_BASED} in the simulator.


\subsection{Density-Aware Gossip}
% Explain:
% - Neighbor density adaptation
% - Congestion mitigation
The third refinement addresses exactly that blindness: the forwarding probability is made inversely dependent on the number of neighbors a node currently observes, following the neighbor-knowledge principle of Tseng et al.~\cite{tseng2002broadcast}. A node surrounded by many peers can afford to stay silent, since the expected number of other relays is high; a node with few neighbors must forward, since it may be an articulation point of the topology. This mechanism attacks the broadcast storm at its root---it decouples the aggregate retransmission count from node density---and proved in simulation to be the single most effective congestion-mitigation ingredient. In isolation, however, it ignores both link quality and the redundancy already observed on the channel.

Each of the three signals---link quality, local density, observed redundancy---thus captures a complementary aspect of the forwarding decision. The natural conclusion is to combine them, which leads to the algorithm at the core of this thesis.

This routing logic is defined as \emph{ADAPTATIVE\_GOSSIP} in the simulator.

\label{sec:sim_smartgossip}

\subsection{Decision Function}
 
Smart Gossip computes, for every newly received packet, a retransmission probability that combines the three signals above as independent multiplicative factors, bounded from below by a survival floor:
 
\begin{equation}
    p \;=\; \max\!\left(p_{\text{floor}},\;\; f_{\text{rssi}} \cdot f_{\text{density}} \cdot f_{\text{dupe}}\right)
    \label{eq:smartgossip}
\end{equation}
 
\noindent with
 
\begin{equation}
    f_{\text{rssi}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\,\alpha\,(\mathrm{RSSI} - \mathrm{RSSI}_{\text{ref}})}}
    \qquad\text{(logistic sigmoid)}
    \label{eq:frssi}
\end{equation}
 
\begin{equation}
    f_{\text{density}} = \frac{1}{1 + \beta\, n}
    \qquad\text{(rational decay, $n$ = current neighbor count)}
    \label{eq:fdensity}
\end{equation}
 
\begin{equation}
    f_{\text{dupe}} = e^{-\gamma\,(c - 1)}
    \qquad\text{(exponential decay, $c$ = copies of the packet received)}
    \label{eq:fdupe}
\end{equation}
 
The node then draws a uniform random number and retransmits if it falls below $p$, after the randomised back-off described in Section~\ref{sec:communication_flow}. All inputs to Equation~\eqref{eq:smartgossip} are locally observable at zero communication cost: RSSI is reported by the transceiver with every reception, the neighbor count is maintained by passive overhearing, and the copy counter is a by-product of the duplicate cache. The algorithm therefore adds no control traffic whatsoever.

\subsection{Rationale of Each Factor}
 
\paragraph{The RSSI factor.} Equation~\eqref{eq:frssi} encodes the edge-of-coverage heuristic introduced above: it approaches 1 as the RSSI approaches the sensitivity limit ($-131.5$~dBm), where the receiver is likely one of very few nodes to have heard the packet, and approaches 0 for strong signals from nearby transmitters whose coverage the receiver cannot meaningfully extend. A sigmoid is preferred over a hard threshold because RSSI in a shadowed channel is a noisy quantity; a continuous transition prevents abrupt behavioural changes from decibel-level fluctuations, and its shape is consistent with the coverage probability induced by log-normal fading~\cite{senel2011relay,cattani2017reliability}. The parameter $\alpha$ controls the steepness of the transition and $\mathrm{RSSI}_{\text{ref}}$ its centre.
 
\paragraph{The density factor.} Equation~\eqref{eq:fdensity} is the direct counter-measure to the broadcast storm~\cite{ni1999broadcast,tseng2002broadcast}: it scales the individual forwarding probability inversely with the local neighbor count $n$, so that the \emph{expected number of relays} in a neighborhood---roughly $n \cdot f_{\text{density}}$, which approaches the constant $1/\beta$ for large $n$---remains bounded as density grows, instead of growing linearly as under flooding. The rational form $1/(1+\beta n)$ is the simplest function with value 1 at $n=0$, smooth decay and a zero asymptote.
 
\paragraph{The duplicate factor.} Equation~\eqref{eq:fdupe} is a continuous reformulation of the counter-based scheme of Tseng et al.~\cite{tseng2002broadcast}: every copy of a packet a node overhears is direct evidence that the packet is already well disseminated in its vicinity, so the incentive to add yet another copy decays exponentially. Unlike the original binary threshold, the exponential form degrades gracefully, halving the probability roughly every two additional copies (for the optimised $\gamma$).
 
\paragraph{The probability floor.} Finally, $p_{\text{floor}}$ guarantees that no combination of inputs ever silences a node completely. It is the safeguard against gossip die-out identified in Section~\ref{sec:gossip}: in pathological cases---a sparse bridge node that happens to receive a strong signal, for instance---the multiplicative factors could otherwise drive $p$ arbitrarily close to zero and sever the network. The floor also provides a single, intuitive operational knob: raising $p_{\text{floor}}$ towards 1 continuously morphs Smart Gossip back into flooding, a property exploited in Section~\ref{sec:sim_final_params}.

Table~\ref{tab:sg_examples} evaluates Equation~\eqref{eq:smartgossip} with the optimised parameters of Section~\ref{sec:sim_optimization} in five representative situations, illustrating how the factors interact. A node at the coverage edge with few neighbors forwards with high probability (0.70); an average node halves that; observed duplicates suppress it further; and well-connected nodes near the transmitter fall to the floor, retransmitting only 11\% of the time.

\subsection{Why Manual Tuning Is Insufficient}
 
Equation~\eqref{eq:smartgossip} introduces five free parameters: $\theta = (\alpha, \mathrm{RSSI}_{\text{ref}}, \beta, \gamma, p_{\text{floor}})$. Owing to their effects are strongly coupled---a steeper sigmoid changes the operating point at which the density factor matters, the duplicate penalty interacts with the floor---so tuning them one at a time is methodologically unsound. Also, exhaustive search fares no better: a modest grid of ten values per dimension yields $10^5$ combinations, each requiring multiple full network simulations to average out stochastic noise. Finally, the objective surface is non-convex and \emph{noisy}, since every evaluation is itself a random experiment, which rules out gradient-based methods. The problem calls for a derivative-free global optimiser that is sample-efficient in moderate dimensions.
 
\subsection{Differential Evolution}
 
Differential evolution (DE)~\cite{storn1997de,price2005de} satisfies these requirements and was adopted through its \texttt{scipy.optimize} implementation~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}. DE maintains a population of candidate parameter vectors and evolves it through three operators. \emph{Mutation} creates, for each individual $\mathbf{x}_i$, a mutant vector from three distinct randomly chosen members of the population,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{x}_a + F \cdot (\mathbf{x}_b - \mathbf{x}_c),
    \label{eq:de_mutation}
\end{equation}
where the differential weight $F$ was dithered in $[0.5, 1.0]$ to balance exploration and convergence~\cite{price2005de}. \emph{Crossover} then mixes $\mathbf{v}_i$ with $\mathbf{x}_i$ coordinate-wise with recombination probability 0.7, producing a trial vector. \emph{Selection} is greedy: the trial replaces $\mathbf{x}_i$ in the next generation only if it achieves a better fitness. Because mutation step sizes derive from the population's own spread, DE self-adapts its search scale, behaving exploratively while the population is dispersed and refining once it concentrates around an optimum---a property well suited to noisy, non-convex landscapes~\cite{das2011de}.
 
\subsection{Fitness Function and Configuration}
 
Each candidate $\theta$ is evaluated by running full network simulations at sizes of 15 and 30 nodes---the regime where the routing decision matters most---over multiple seeded repetitions, and aggregating four metrics into the scalar fitness
\begin{equation}
    \text{fitness}(\theta) = 0.40\,\overline{\text{reach}} + 0.40\,\overline{\text{useful}} - 0.10\,\overline{\text{col}} - 0.10\,\overline{\text{tx}},
    \label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{\text{reach}}$ is the mean reachability, $\overline{\text{useful}}$ the mean usefulness, $\overline{\text{col}}$ the mean collision rate (all in \%) and $\overline{\text{tx}}$ the mean per-node transmission airtime normalised to a comparable scale. The weights encode the design intent: coverage and channel efficiency are rewarded equally and dominantly, while collisions and raw airtime act as soft penalties. The search ranges of the five parameters (Table~\ref{tab:sg_params}) were chosen generously around physically meaningful values. The optimisation converged after approximately 160 fitness evaluations, completing in under ten minutes of computation---itself a demonstration of why the exploration had to be conducted in simulation.


\subsection{Limitations of the Optimization}
 
Three limitations of this process must be acknowledged. First, to keep the campaign tractable, each fitness evaluation averaged over a small number of repetitions, so the optimum is estimated under residual noise; a larger budget would yield a more robust optimum. Second, fitness was evaluated only at 15 and 30 nodes; including the extremes (5 and 50) might have shifted the optimum towards higher coverage in small networks. Third, Equation~\eqref{eq:fitness} does not impose a hard reachability constraint, which explains the coverage deficit observed in the smallest networks in Section~\ref{sec:sim_results}. These limitations delimit the validity of the chosen parameters and directly motivate the future-work directions of Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}.


\section{Performance Metrics}
\label{sec:sim_metrics}
 
Four metrics, computed identically for every algorithm and scenario, quantify the quality of a forwarding policy. Together they capture the two sides of the fundamental trade-off---how much of the network is reached, and at what cost in channel resources.
 
\subsection{Reachability}
 
Reachability is the fraction of origin--destination pairs for which a generated message was correctly delivered, expressed as the number of useful receptions over $M\,(N-1)$, where $M$ is the number of generated messages and $N$ the network size. It is the primary effectiveness metric: it answers whether the network performs its function. Absolute values must be interpreted against the topology; in the sparse 15$\times$15~km scenarios simulated here, some node pairs lack physical connectivity altogether, bounding reachability below 100\% for \emph{any} algorithm.
 
\subsection{Average Delay}
 
Delay is measured per delivered message as the time elapsed between generation at the source application and reception at the destination, and averaged over all deliveries. Beyond user experience, delay is a sensitive \emph{congestion indicator}: in a listen-before-talk MAC, channel saturation manifests as packets waiting tens of seconds for clear air, so a diverging delay curve reveals a collapsing channel even when delivery eventually succeeds. Note that the metric is conditioned on delivery; messages never delivered do not contribute.
 
\subsection{Transmission Air Utilization}
 
Air utilization is the total time each node's radio spends transmitting over the simulation, averaged per node. It is the direct cost metric of a forwarding policy and carries a double physical meaning: it is proportional to the radio's energy expenditure---linking the protocol to the power requirement of Section~\ref{subsec:req_power}---and it measures consumption of the regulatory duty-cycle budget of the EU~868 band, the hard ceiling identified in Section~\ref{subsec:req_scalability}.
 
\subsection{Usefulness}
 
Usefulness is the fraction of received packets that carried new information to their receiver, i.e.\ that were not duplicates already present in its cache. It is the \emph{efficiency} metric of the channel: a low value means the medium is being filled with copies nobody needs. Usefulness isolates precisely the redundancy that gossip is designed to remove, making it the most discriminating single indicator of forwarding intelligence. The collision rate---the fraction of sensed packets destroyed by concurrent transmissions---is additionally reported as a secondary congestion indicator.

\subsection{Channel Efficiency}
 
The most robust result of the campaign is the reduction in air utilization: for networks of 15 nodes and above, Smart Gossip occupies 27--32\% less channel time per node than managed flood, with small variance across 30 repetitions. The practical significance is twofold. In energy terms, transmission is the dominant consumer in a LoRa node, so this reduction translates directly into battery lifetime. In regulatory terms, with the \texttt{LONG\_FAST} airtime of ${\sim}550$~ms per packet, managed flood at 15 nodes already consumes 5.4\% of duty cycle over the 30-minute window against Smart Gossip's 3.8\%; under heavier traffic or larger networks, flooding approaches the regulatory ceiling first, at which point nodes are legally forced to silence. The usefulness metric explains where the savings come from: under managed flood, fewer than half of all received packets carry new information, whereas Smart Gossip raises usefulness by a consistent ${\sim}10\%$ relative for all networks of 15 nodes and above---the same information is disseminated with substantially fewer transmissions.
 
\subsection{Congestion Collapse under Flooding}
 
The delay results expose the broadcast storm in its most dramatic form. At 30 nodes, managed flood exhibits a mean delay of 78.6~s---and 160~s at 50 nodes---against 11.4~s and 27.1~s for Smart Gossip, reductions of 85\% and 83\% respectively. The mechanism is the one anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:gossip}: when every node contends to retransmit, the listen-before-talk MAC forces packets to queue for tens of seconds awaiting clear channel, and collisions force further retries. Smart Gossip removes the bulk of this contention at the source by silencing redundant relays, so the packets that are forwarded traverse a comparatively idle channel. The collision-rate results corroborate the picture at 5--30 nodes (reductions of 18--37\%); only at 50 nodes does managed flood show marginally fewer collisions ($+1.5$~pp for Smart Gossip), an artefact of its channel being so saturated that its contention windows serialise transmissions---at the cost of the 160-second delays just described.
 
\subsection{The Coverage Trade-off and Failure Scenarios}
 
The cost of these gains is a reachability deficit of 4.5--12.4 percentage points, largest at intermediate sizes. This is the inherent gossip trade-off anticipated theoretically~\cite{haas2006gossip}: every suppressed retransmission is also a suppressed redundancy opportunity, and in a sparse topology some of those opportunities were load-bearing. Three observations contextualise the deficit. First, absolute reachability is bounded by physical connectivity in these sparse scenarios---both algorithms range from ${\sim}30$ to ${\sim}77\%$---so part of the gap reflects marginal links that only brute-force redundancy can cross. Second, the deficit \emph{shrinks} in the largest network ($-4.5$~pp at 50 nodes) while the efficiency advantages persist, indicating that the trade-off improves exactly where it matters. Third, the deficit is largest precisely in the configurations excluded from the optimisation fitness, suggesting it is partly an artefact of the tuning procedure rather than of the algorithm itself.
 
The clear failure scenario for Smart Gossip is the small, sparse network. At 5 nodes, congestion is a non-problem---there is nothing to optimise away---while every retransmission matters for coverage; managed flood is the better algorithm there, also winning the composite comparison below and incurring lower delay ($+28.7\%$ for Smart Gossip). Conversely, the failure scenario for managed flood is density: its delay and airtime diverge with network size, which is incompatible with the scalability requirement of Section~\ref{subsec:req_scalability}.
 
\subsection{Composite Comparison}
 
To aggregate the four metrics into a single comparative figure, a composite score was defined weighing effectiveness and efficiency equally and penalising channel costs:
\begin{equation}
    \text{score} = 0.35\,\text{reach} + 0.35\,\text{useful} - 0.15\,\text{col} - 0.15\,\frac{\text{tx}_{\text{air}}}{1000}.
    \label{eq:composite}
\end{equation}
The weighting differs slightly from the optimisation fitness of Equation~\eqref{eq:fitness}, placing heavier emphasis on channel costs, precisely to verify that the optimised parameters do not merely overfit their own objective. Table~\ref{tab:res_score} shows the outcome: Smart Gossip wins in three of the four network sizes, with an advantage that grows with density, and loses only in the 5-node scenario where coverage dominates.

\section{Hardware Requirements}
\label{sec:hw_requirements}

The system-level requirements established in Section~\ref{sec:design_requirements}
translate into a concrete set of hardware constraints that shaped every design
decision in the custom printed circuit board (PCB). Four principal constraints govern
the design space.

\paragraph{Autonomy.}
Because of the device is intended for deployment scenarios in which mains power is unavailable
for extended periods, together with a target runtime of several days of continuous mesh operation
on a single lithium polymer (LiPo) cell demands that the combined average current
draw of the microcontroller, LoRa transceiver, BLE radio, and all ancillary
circuitry remains in the range of a few milliamperes.

\paragraph{Compactness.}
The node is designed to be carried alongside a smartphone, so the PCB form factor
must be kept to a minimum consistent with the required component area. In fact, the resulting
board measures 74.5\,mm\,$\times$\,89.8\,mm, a footprint that accommodates the
integrated PCB antenna, the USB-C connector, the battery header, and all active
circuitry without requiring multi-board stacking.

\paragraph{RF compliance.}
Operation in the EU\,868\,MHz ISM band is governed by ETSI EN\,300\,220~\cite{etsi2020en300220};
most sub-bands within 863--870\,MHz impose a maximum radiated power of 25\,mW
(14\,dBm ERP) and a duty-cycle ceiling of 1\,\%. The firmware enforces the
duty-cycle budget in software (Chapter~\ref{ch:firmware}), while the RF subsystem
is sized to remain within the radiated limits achievable by the on-board PCB antenna
at the configured transmit power.

\paragraph{Cost and manufacturability.}
As a prototype research platform, the design targets standard two-layer PCB
fabrication and commercially available, off-the-shelf components sourced from LCSC.
No exotic materials, controlled-impedance inner layers, or hand-wound inductors are
required. The complete bill of materials contains fewer than thirty distinct line
items, all available in standard SMD packages ranging from 0402 to SOIC-16.

\subsection{Battery Selection}
\label{subsec:hw_battery}

A single-cell lithium polymer cell was selected as the energy source. Lithium polymer
chemistry offers an energy density superior to nickel-metal hydride or alkaline
alternatives, a relatively flat discharge curve that simplifies state-of-charge

protection electronics~\cite{nanjing2023tp4056}. The single-cell topology (nominal

with the TP4056 charger and the TPS63001 buck-boost regulator without requiring
series cell-balancing circuits. The cell is connected through a two-pin JST-compatible
header (J1, mounted on the board underside) so that different cell capacities can
be fitted to the same platform without hardware changes.

\subsection{Charging Circuit}
\label{subsec:hw_charger}

Charging is handled by the TP4056-42 (U4), a fully integrated constant-current
constant-voltage linear charger for single-cell LiPo and Li-ion batteries in an
ESOP-8 package with an exposed thermal pad~\cite{nanjing2023tp4056}. The IC accepts
an input voltage between 4.0\,V and 8.0\,V, making it directly compatible with the
USB\,5\,V supply, and terminates charging at 4.2\,V with $\pm$1\,\% accuracy. A
500\,mA polyfuse (F1, 1812 package) is inserted in series with the VBUS rail upstream
of the charger to protect the USB supply from a downstream overcurrent fault.

The charge current is programmed by a single resistor connected between the PROG pin
and ground. With R6\,$=$\,6.8\,k$\Omega$ alone (solder jumper JP1 open), the
resulting charge current is approximately 130\,mA---conservative by design, imposing
no thermal demands on the compact package. Additionally, an optional solder jumper JP1 places a
second resistor in parallel with R6, reducing the equivalent PROG resistance and
raising the charge current to approximately 300\,mA for applications where faster
turnaround is preferred. Furthermore, the open-collector $\overline{\text{CHRG}}$ and
$\overline{\text{STDBY}}$ outputs of the TP4056 drive Q1 and Q2 (SS8050 NPN
transistors) through 1\,k$\Omega$ current-limiting resistors, which in turn
illuminate D1 (red) during active charging and D2 (green) when the cell has reached
full charge.

Last but not least, a Schottky diode D3 (SS34, SMA package, 3\,A rated) is placed in the power path to
prevent reverse conduction from the battery back into the USB supply when the latter
is absent or at a lower potential than the battery, ensuring that the system can
be powered from either source without contention between the two rails.

\subsection{Voltage Regulation}
\label{subsec:hw_regulator}

The regulated 3.3\,V rail is generated by the Texas Instruments TPS63001 (U8), a
synchronous buck-boost converter in the DRC0010J leadless package with an exposed
thermal pad~\cite{ti2012tps63001}. The choice of a buck-boost topology is dictated
by the battery voltage window: a freshly charged LiPo cell delivers 4.2\,V
(requiring step-down conversion), while a partially discharged cell may present
3.3\,V or less (requiring step-up conversion). Hence, a purely buck or purely boost
converter would lose regulation at one extreme of this range, while the TPS63001
transitions seamlessly between both modes as the cell discharges, maintaining a
regulated 3.3\,V across the full battery window from approximately 1.8\,V to 5.5\,V
input.

The TPS63001 operates at a fixed switching frequency of approximately 1.5\,MHz,
which allows the external inductor (L1, 2.2\,$\mu$H, 1008 package) and output
capacitors to be kept small. In addition, the converter's typical peak efficiency exceeds 90\,\%
across the battery's mid-discharge range~\cite{ti2012tps63001}, a critical figure for
meeting the multi-day autonomy target. Following the converter output, two bulk capacitors (C12, 100\,$\mu$F, 1206;
C13, 2.2\,$\mu$F, 1206) are placed, complemented by
distributed 100\,nF and 10\,$\mu$F decoupling capacitors close to the supply pins of
all active devices.

\subsection{Power Monitoring}
\label{subsec:hw_batmon}

In order to monitor the state of charge of the LiPo cell, the firmware measures its terminal
voltage through a resistive divider connected to one of the ESP32's 12-bit analogue
inputs. Given that the resistors R13 (330\,k$\Omega$) and R14 (470\,k$\Omega$) form a divider with
ratio $470/(330+470)\approx0.59$, scaling the 3.0--4.2\,V battery range to
approximately 1.76--2.47\,V, comfortably within the 0--3.3\,V ADC input range of
the ESP32~\cite{espressif2023esp32trm}. Thus, the firmware applies a multiplicative
correction factor to reconstruct the actual battery voltage from the sampled reading,
and reports a low-battery warning when the reconstructed voltage falls below 3.0\,V.
The high total impedance of the divider ($\approx$800\,k$\Omega$) ensures that the
monitoring path dissipates less than 6\,$\mu$W at full charge, with a negligible
impact on the overall energy budget.

The ESP32's integrated 2.4\,GHz radio supports Bluetooth 4.2, including the BLE

\subsection{Memory Considerations}
\label{subsec:hw_memory}

The 4\,MB flash within the module is partitioned into a bootloader region, the
primary firmware image, and a non-volatile storage (NVS) partition that persists
configuration parameters. The 520\,KB of on-chip SRAM is shared
between the FreeRTOS task stacks, the Bluedroid BLE host stack, and the application
data structures, including the duplicate packet cache and the neighbour table. Under the profiling conducted during
firmware development, peak heap occupancy remained below 200\,KB, leaving a margin
sufficient for future protocol extensions without requiring external SRAM.


\section{RF Subsystem}
\label{sec:hw_rf}

\img{images/rf_subsystem.png}{Schematic of the RF subsystem, showing the RFM95W LoRa module.}{0.6}{hw_rf}

\subsection{LoRa Transceiver Selection}
\label{subsec:hw_lora}

The LoRa radio is implemented by the HopeRF RFM95W-868S2 (U2), a compact SMD module
measuring 16\,mm\,$\times$\,17\,mm that integrates the Semtech SX1276 transceiver
die, a TCXO-grade crystal oscillator, RF matching network, and RF switch within a
shielded metal enclosure~\cite{hoperf2018rfm95w, semtech2019sx1276}. The SX1276 is
the same silicon underpinning the Meshtastic platform reviewed in
Section~\ref{subsec:meshtastic}, ensuring PHY-level compatibility with its packet
format and enabling interoperability tests between A3MESH nodes and commercial
Meshtastic hardware. The module operates from a 3.3\,V supply and draws a transmit
current of approximately 120\,mA at maximum output power in PA\_BOOST mode, and a
receive current of approximately 10.3\,mA~\cite{semtech2019sx1276}; these figures
directly inform the firmware duty-cycling strategy.

The module exposes a 16-pad castellated interface providing power supply, SPI bus,
interrupt outputs, and an RF port and the RF port connects directly
to the integrated PCB antenna, forgoing an external coaxial connector and its
associated insertion loss.

\subsection{Antenna Design Considerations}
\label{subsec:hw_antenna}

The RF radiator (AE1) is an integrated PCB monopole antenna implemented according
to Texas Instruments Design Note DN024~\cite{ti_dn024}, which documents a compact
meandered monopole geometry validated for 868\,MHz, 915\,MHz, and 955\,MHz
operation. The use of a printed antenna eliminates the cost, mechanical complexity,
and failure mode of a coaxial connector, and is consistent with the compact
form factor of the device. The same antenna geometry is widely deployed in
commercially produced ISM-band sensor nodes, making it a well-characterised,
industrially proven choice for this application.

A quarter-wavelength monopole resonant at 868\,MHz would require a straight conductor
of approximately 86\,mm in free space. The DN024 geometry compresses this electrical
length into a meandered copper trace occupying a footprint of 38\,mm\,$\times$\,24\,mm
by folding the radiating element above a segmented ground plane, whose sections act
as the return-current mirror and determine the radiation pattern and input impedance.
The antenna's single feed pad connects directly to the RFM95W RF output pad, which
includes the on-module matching network that presents a 50\,$\Omega$ interface to the
antenna feed point.

\subsection{RF Matching and Layout}
\label{subsec:hw_rf_layout}

A keep-out zone of 38\,mm\,$\times$\,24\,mm is enforced on both copper layers beneath
the antenna footprint; no vias, signal traces, or power planes encroach on this
region, as partial copper coverage within the keep-out degrades the radiation pattern
and lowers antenna efficiency~\cite{ti_dn024}. The SPI bus traces between the ESP32
and the RFM95W are routed on the front copper layer as short, direct connections
to minimise series resistance and stray capacitance at the 10\,MHz SPI clock
frequency. All power supply traces to the RFM95W and ESP32 are supplemented with
local 100\,nF decoupling capacitors placed within 0.5\,mm of each supply pin.

\subsection{Regulatory Constraints}
\label{subsec:hw_regulatory}

Operation in the EU\,868\,MHz band is subject to ETSI EN\,300\,220, which
subdivides the 863--870\,MHz range into sub-bands with individual power and
duty-cycle limits~\cite{etsi2020en300220, adelantado2017limits}. The majority of
these sub-bands permit a maximum ERP of 14\,dBm (25\,mW) with a 1\,\% duty cycle.
At a transmitter output power of 20\,dBm and a PCB antenna with gain of
approximately 0\,dBi, the resulting EIRP marginally exceeds the 14\,dBm sub-band
limit of the standard channels.\footnote{Sub-band 869.4--869.65\,MHz is an
exception under ETSI EN\,300\,220, permitting up to 500\,mW ERP with a 10\,\%
duty cycle~\cite{etsi2020en300220}; the 20\,dBm transmit level of the RFM95W
falls within this limit. Formal EMC testing and a Declaration of Conformity are
outside the scope of this prototype platform.} The firmware's duty-cycle enforcement
subsystem (Chapter~\ref{ch:firmware}) tracks the cumulative airtime over a sliding
one-hour window and prevents the node from transmitting beyond the applicable
regulatory budget, irrespective of the configured RF power level.


\section{USB Programming Interface}
\label{sec:hw_usb}

\img{images/prog_subsystem.png}{Schematic of the USB programming and user interface subsystem, showing the CH340C USB-to-UART bridge and auto-reset circuit.}{0.7}{hw_usb}

Firmware development and debugging require reliable access to the ESP32's UART
bootloader without manual intervention. This is accomplished by pairing the CH340C
(U7) USB-to-UART bridge with a two-transistor auto-reset circuit.

The CH340C is a crystal-less USB-to-UART converter in a SOIC-16 package that
derives its clock from the USB host bus, eliminating the tolerance sensitivity and
cost of an external crystal~\cite{ch340c2023}. Its UART output is connected to the
ESP32's default UART0 (GPIO pins 1 and 3), which serves both as the firmware
programming channel and as the debug serial console at 115\,200\,baud.

The auto-reset circuit replicates the approach used on standard ESP32 development
boards: the DTR and RTS control lines of the CH340C drive the base terminals of two
SS8050 NPN transistors (Q1, Q2) through 100\,$\Omega$ base resistors (R12, R8). The
collectors are connected to the BOOT (GPIO0) and EN (reset) pins of the ESP32
respectively, each with a 10\,k$\Omega$ pull-up to 3.3\,V. When a host initiates a
programming sequence, the USB-to-serial driver asserts a specific DTR/RTS toggle
pattern that pulls EN low to reset the ESP32, then holds GPIO0 low to latch it into
bootloader mode upon reset release---all without the user pressing any button.
Manual override remains available through SW1 (BOOT) and SW2 (RST) tactile switches.

The USB interface uses a 16-pin USB-C receptacle (J6, GCT USB4105 series) supporting
USB 2.0 signals only. Two 5.1\,k$\Omega$ resistors (R15, R16) pull down the CC1 and
CC2 lines, which is the USB Type-C specification's mechanism for a device to signal
its power sink requirements to a compliant charger or host port.


\section{PCB Design}
\label{sec:hw_pcb}

The board was designed in KiCad\,9.0 and manufactured on a standard two-layer FR4
laminate, 1.6\,mm nominal thickness. The copper stackup comprises a front signal
layer (F.Cu) carrying the majority of the active components, and a back copper layer
(B.Cu) used as a return-current reference and ground plane beneath the low-frequency
digital circuitry.

Component placement follows a functional partitioning strategy. The power subsystem
occupies the lower-left quadrant, with the TP4056, protection MOSFETs, and TPS63001
grouped to minimise the area of the high-frequency switching node of the buck-boost
converter; the 2.2\,$\mu$H inductor (L1) is placed as close as possible to the
TPS63001's switching pins to reduce the current loop area and the associated
electromagnetic emission. The ESP32 module is centred on the board, with SPI bus
traces to the RFM95W module kept short. The CH340C and auto-reset transistors are
clustered near the USB-C connector to minimise the length of the USB differential
pair D+/D-.

The antenna occupies the top-right corner of the board, with the 38\,mm\,$\times$\,24\,mm
keep-out zone maintained free of copper on both layers. The battery connector J1 is
placed on the underside of the board to allow the cable to exit without obstruction.

Decoupling follows a two-tier strategy: 100\,nF X5R capacitors in 0402 packages are
placed within 0.5\,mm of each active device supply pin to suppress switching
transients, while 10\,$\mu$F bulk capacitors distributed across the board buffer
medium-frequency current demand. The 100\,$\mu$F output capacitor of the TPS63001
provides low-frequency reservoir capacity.


\section{Prototype Manufacturing}
\label{sec:hw_manufacturing}

The KiCad layout was exported to Gerber files in RS-274-X format together with NC drill files, which were then submitted to JLCPCB for fabrication of a two-layer PCB. All SMD passives use 0402 or 0603 packages, except for bulk capacitors, which require the larger 1206 package to accommodate their voltage ratings. Integrated circuits range from SOT-23-6 to SOIC-16, while the USB-C receptacle and battery header are semi-through-hole parts, chosen for the mechanical robustness they provide against repeated connector cycles.

Furthermore, the BOM was compiled with LCSC part numbers so that JLCPCB could source every component from a single distributor, streamlining assembly. Finally, seven test-point pads were distributed across the board to facilitate in-circuit measurement of the key power rails (VBUS, 3.3\,V, BAT+) and programming signals during bringup.

\chapter{Firmware Architecture and Implementation}
\label{ch:firmware}

This chapter describes the design philosophy, the layered decomposition, and the concrete implementation of each
subsystem, drawing directly from the \texttt{tfg\_firmware/firmware} PlatformIO
project.


\section{Firmware Design Philosophy}
\label{sec:fw_philosophy}

Three principles govern the design of the A3MESH firmware: strict layer separation,
non-blocking interrupt handling, and static memory allocation.

\paragraph{Strict layer separation.} Each software layer communicates with
adjacent layers exclusively through FreeRTOS queues; no layer directly invokes
functions from a non-adjacent layer. The sole deliberate exception is the C-linkage
wrapper in \texttt{PassiveACK.h}, which allows the LoRa transmit task---residing in
the \texttt{lora} PlatformIO library directory---to call into the mesh layer without
creating a circular header dependency between library directories.

\paragraph{Non-blocking interrupt handling.} Follows the deferred-processing pattern
standard in FreeRTOS embedded designs~\cite{freertos2024}. In particular, the SX1276's DIO0 pin
asserts on RxDone and triggers an ISR declared \texttt{IRAM\_ATTR}, so that it
executes from instruction RAM regardless of flash cache state. Especially, the ISR does nothing but
release a binary semaphore and optionally request a context switch. Apart from that all SPI transfers
and frame parsing happen in a dedicated high-priority task, ensuring that the radio
FIFO is emptied before a subsequent preamble can corrupt it.

\paragraph{Static memory allocation.} Eliminates heap fragmentation and non-deterministic
latency on the critical path. For instance, a pool of eight statically allocated frame slots
accommodates bursts of arriving frames, then inter-task transfers pass a 32-bit pointer to
a pool slot rather than copying the payload, reducing queue-transfer overhead to a
single word. As a result, no dynamic allocation occurs on any path between frame reception and
forwarding decision.


\section{Layered Architecture}
\label{sec:fw_layers}

The firmware is decomposed into four horizontal layers. Firstly, the
\emph{Physical Communication Layer} encapsulates the LoRa and BLE radio interfaces.
Secondly, the \emph{Mesh Logic Layer} implements packet validation, duplicate suppression,
neighbour management, and the Smart Gossip forwarding algorithm. Thirdly, the
\emph{System Services Layer} handles regulatory duty-cycle compliance, battery
state estimation, and periodic housekeeping. Finally, the \emph{Application and Interface
Layer} exposes an AT-command interface over BLE to the companion mobile application
and manages the bidirectional data pipelines between radio and smartphone.

Seven FreeRTOS tasks are created at boot time. Four are pinned to Core~0, dedicated
to all radio activity; three are pinned to Core~1, which handles BLE and pipeline
processing (Table~\ref{tab:fw_tasks}).

    \centering

Four queues provide all inter-layer communication:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{xLoRaRxQueue} carries pointers to pool-allocated frame descriptors from
    the radio ISR handler to the mesh pipeline.
    \item \texttt{xLoRaTxQueue} carries outbound frame descriptors from the mesh and
    application layers to the radio transmitter.
    \item \texttt{xBLERxQueue} carries AT-command strings from the BLE stack to the command
    processor.
    \item \texttt{xBLETxQueue} carries AT response and notification strings from any task
    towards the BLE notification sender.
\end{itemize}
All queues have a depth of ten items and are statically allocated at initialisation.

\section{Physical Communication Layer}
\label{sec:fw_physical}

\subsection{LoRa Driver}
\label{sec:fw_lora_driver}

The LoRa driver wraps the Arduino-LoRa SPI library with an interrupt-deferred
receive path, a static frame pool, and regulatory airtime accounting. It is
instantiated as a global singleton in \texttt{main.cpp} and configured at boot with
the parameters defined in \texttt{config.h}: 868\,MHz carrier, spreading factor~8,
bandwidth 125\,kHz, coding rate 4/5, 20\,dBm transmit power via PA\_BOOST, sync
word~0x12, and a preamble of eight symbols. These values yield a raw data rate of
approximately 3.1\,kbit/s and a practical ground-level range of several kilometres
while keeping the time-on-air of a maximum-length frame below one second.

\paragraph{Receive path.}
The SX1276 DIO0 line is connected to GPIO26 and configured as a rising-edge
interrupt. When it fires, the ISR \texttt{onLoRaRxIRQ()}, placed in IRAM, calls
\texttt{xSemaphoreGiveFromISR()} and, if this unblocks a higher-priority task,
sets \texttt{xHigherPriorityTaskWoken} accordingly. This semaphore is what
\texttt{vLoRaRxTask} is waiting on: running at priority~2 on Core~0, the task
blocks indefinitely on the corresponding \texttt{xSemaphoreTake()} until the ISR
wakes it. Once woken, it first calls \texttt{LoRa.parsePacket()} to confirm frame
availability and obtain its length, then allocates a pool slot via
\texttt{alloc\_frame()} using a volatile round-robin index~\cite[Ch.~2]{tanenbaum2015modern}, and reads the payload
bytes with \texttt{LoRa.readBytes()}. Along the way it also records the RSSI,
before re-entering continuous-receive mode with \texttt{LoRa.receive()} and
finally posting the pool-slot pointer to \texttt{xLoRaRxQueue}. Throughout this
path, no copying takes place: the bytes written by the SPI driver into the pool
slot are the same bytes later consumed by the mesh pipeline.

\paragraph{Transmit path.}
\texttt{vLoRaTxTask} blocks on \texttt{xLoRaTxQueue}, waiting for items to dequeue.
Each dequeued item is a \texttt{tx\_LoRa\_msg\_t} descriptor containing the payload
bytes, a jitter delay in milliseconds, a packet identifier, the original source
address, and a forwarding flag. The task then calls \texttt{vTaskDelay()} for the
specified jitter period, which in turn creates the window during which a passive
ACK cancellation may suppress the transmission (Section~\ref{sec:fw_passive_ack}).
Once this delay elapses, it calls \texttt{mesh\_consume\_pending\_forward()}: if the
forward has been cancelled in the meantime, the descriptor is simply discarded;
otherwise, \texttt{check\_and\_record\_airtime()} is invoked first, followed by
\texttt{lora.send\_raw\_pkt(data,\,len)}.

\paragraph{Duty-cycle enforcement.}
Under the EU868 general sub-band, the maximum allowed on-air time is 1\,\% per
hour~\cite{etsi2020en300220}, or equivalently 36\,000\,ms per rolling 3600-second
window. To enforce this limit, the driver maintains a ring buffer of 20
transmission records (\texttt{\_airtime\_log}), where each entry holds a timestamp
together with the airtime estimated by \texttt{compute\_airtime\_ms(pkt\_len)} for
that packet. Before each transmission, \texttt{check\_and\_record\_airtime()} sums
the airtime of all entries whose timestamp falls within the last 3600\,s; if adding
the pending packet would then push the total above 36\,000\,ms, the transmission is
blocked silently. By design, this sliding window avoids the compliance gaps that a
fixed-period counter would otherwise introduce around midnight or after a device
restart.

\subsection{BLE Driver}
\label{sec:fw_ble_driver}

The BLE driver implements a Nordic UART Service (NUS)-compatible GATT server using
the ESP-IDF Bluedroid stack~\cite{espressif2023espidf}. In particular, a single
GATT service hosts two characteristics: a TX characteristic with the
\textsc{Notify} property, used to push data from node to smartphone, and an RX
characteristic with the \textsc{Write Without Response} property, used by the
smartphone to send AT commands. This two-characteristic layout is in fact the
standard for transparent serial emulation over BLE, and as such is natively
supported by serial terminal applications across Android and
iOS~\cite{bluetooth2023core}.

Connection lifecycle, in turn, is managed by \texttt{ServerCallbacks}:
\texttt{onConnect} sets the internal \texttt{\_deviceConnected} flag, while
\texttt{onDisconnect} clears it and immediately restarts advertising, so that the
node remains always connectable without requiring a reboot. When the smartphone
writes to the RX characteristic, \texttt{RXCallbacks::onWrite()} copies the
received value into a 256-byte buffer and then posts it to \texttt{xBLERxQueue} via
a non-blocking \texttt{xQueueSend}; if the queue happens to be full, indicating
transient saturation of the command processor, the frame is dropped rather than
blocking the Bluedroid callback thread. Outbound data, meanwhile, travels the
reverse path: \texttt{vBLETxTask} on Core~1 drains \texttt{xBLETxQueue} and, for
each dequeued string, calls \texttt{ble.send()}, thereby issuing a GATT
notification to the connected peer. By decoupling BLE transmission into a
dedicated task, long notify round trips are prevented from stalling time-sensitive
state-machine transitions.

The four frame types defined by \texttt{a3mesh\_msg\_type\_t} are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{k\_data\_plain}: unencrypted user message.
    \item \texttt{k\_discovery\_type}: neighbour beacon.
    \item \texttt{k\_key\_exchange}: in-band key provisioning.
    \item \texttt{k\_data\_encrypted}: AES-256-GCM ciphertext; the encryption scope is further
encoded in the \texttt{type} sub-field as \texttt{a3mesh\_enc\_type\_t}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{k\_enc\_node2node}: unicast between two nodes
        \item \texttt{k\_enc\_group}: multicast to a group of nodes sharing the same key
        \item \texttt{k\_enc\_network}: broadcast to all nodes in the network
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Serialisation, performed by \texttt{A3MESHPacket::serialize\_header\_pkt()}, fills
the header's \texttt{packet\_length} field automatically and writes both structures
into a caller-provided buffer in packed order. Deserialisation, by
\texttt{A3MESHPacket::deserialize\_pkt()}, applies four integrity checks before
accepting a frame: minimum buffer length against \texttt{HEADER\_SIZE}, buffer
length against the declared \texttt{packet\_length}, payload length against
\texttt{A3MESH\_MAX\_PAYLOAD}, and CRC-16/CCITT verification of the payload; any
failing check discards the frame without further processing. The node identifier
used in \texttt{src} and \texttt{node\_id}, in turn, is derived from the factory
Bluetooth MAC address as described in Section~\ref{sec:fw_identity}.


\section{Mesh Logic Layer}
\label{sec:fw_mesh}

\subsection{Reception State Machine}
\label{sec:fw_statemachine}

All received frames are processed by a ten-state explicit finite state machine
(\texttt{MeshRxState}) running inside \texttt{LoRa2BLETask}. Every frame
traverses exactly one path from \texttt{k\_rx\_idle} to \texttt{k\_rx\_done}; the
only branching points are the validate, classify, data-process, and forward handlers.
The states are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:fw_states}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lp{9.5cm}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{State} & \textbf{Action} \\
        \midrule
        \texttt{k\_rx\_idle}              & Block on \texttt{xLoRaRxQueue}; dequeue pool pointer on arrival. \\
        \texttt{k\_rx\_validate}          & Deserialise; apply self-source filter, TTL check, duplicate cache lookup, and passive-ACK cancel. \\
        \texttt{k\_rx\_classify}          & Route by \texttt{header.type} to the appropriate handler state. \\
        \texttt{k\_rx\_process\_discovery}& Update neighbour table; notify BLE on new neighbour discovery. \\
        \texttt{k\_rx\_process\_data}     & Check destination; decide local delivery and/or gossip forwarding. \\
        \texttt{k\_rx\_process\_key}      & Check destination; deliver key-exchange payload to BLE locally. \\
        \texttt{k\_rx\_local\_delivery}   & Decrypt if encrypted; format and post AT notification to \texttt{xBLETxQueue}. \\
        \texttt{k\_rx\_forward}           & Patch \texttt{node\_id}; decrement TTL; compute jitter; register pending forward; enqueue to \texttt{xLoRaTxQueue}. \\

        \texttt{k\_rx\_done}              & Release pool slot; return to \texttt{k\_rx\_idle}. \\

\paragraph{Packet validation.}
First, at \texttt{k\_rx\_validate}, four guards are applied in order: a self-source filter
which drops any frame whose \texttt{src} matches the local node ID, preventing a node
from processing its own transmissions returning via a relay. Then, a TTL check discards
frames with \texttt{ttl}~$= 0$. Next, the duplicate cache is then consulted via
\texttt{get\_copies()}: if the (\texttt{pkt\_id},~\texttt{src}) pair has been seen
more than \texttt{GOSSIP\_MAX\_COPIES}~$= 3$ times, the frame transitions to
\texttt{k\_rx\_drop}. Finally, \texttt{cancel\_pending\_forward()} is called
unconditionally for every valid frame that matches a message in the cache: a node
that has already queued a forward of this packet and now observes a neighbour
retransmitting it cancels its own copy before it reaches the antenna
(Section~\ref{sec:fw_passive_ack}).

\paragraph{Packet classification.}
Following validation---at \texttt{k\_rx\_classify}---the state machine branches to one of three handlers based on the \texttt{header.type} field.

\paragraph{Data packet handling.}
At \texttt{k\_rx\_process\_data}, broadcast and locally-addressed frames proceed to
\texttt{k\_rx\_local\_delivery} only when \texttt{copies}~$= 1$, preventing the user
from receiving the same message twice via different relay paths. Frames carrying the
\texttt{DIRECT\_UNICAST\_FLAG} in the \texttt{flags} byte are not gossiped; all other
data frames enter \texttt{should\_forward()} for the probabilistic forwarding
decision.

At \texttt{k\_rx\_forward}, the forwarding node patches \texttt{node\_id} with its
own identifier and decrements \texttt{ttl} by one. An RSSI-based jitter delay is then
computed with the linear mapping consistent with the
distance-aware relay selection studied in~\cite{senel2011relay}:
\[
    d_{\mathrm{jitter}} = \mathrm{map}(\mathrm{RSSI},\; -120,\; -40,\; 20,\; 200)\;\;\mathrm{[ms]}
\]

\subsection{Neighbour Discovery}
\label{sec:fw_discovery}

The mesh layer maintains a neighbour table of up to \texttt{MAX\_NEIGHBORS}~$= 10$
entries, each recording the 32-bit node identifier, the most recently observed RSSI,
and the timestamp of the latest reception; entries expire after
\texttt{NODE\_TIMEOUT}~$= 180$\,s of silence.

\texttt{vSendDiscoveryTask} injects beacons, waking every
\texttt{DISCOVERY\_INTERVAL\_MS}~$= 30$\,s plus a random offset of up to 10\,s,
which desynchronises simultaneous power-ons and reduces collisions between
concurrent DISCOVERY transmissions from neighbouring nodes. Each beacon carries
\texttt{ttl}~$= \texttt{MIN\_TTL}~= 1$, limiting propagation to a single hop so that
the table reflects only directly reachable nodes. On reception, the validate state
updates the corresponding entry; if the node is new, \texttt{k\_rx\_process\_discovery}
also posts a \texttt{+DISC=\tcmp{id}} notification to \texttt{xBLETxQueue} to inform
the companion application.

Stale entries are evicted by \texttt{vNeighborCheckTask}, which uses
\texttt{vTaskDelayUntil()} for drift-free periodicity at a wakeup interval of
$\texttt{NODE\_TIMEOUT} / 10$\,ms. Its eviction function \texttt{rm\_tout\_neigbhors()}
applies swap-last deletion---overwriting the expired slot with the last table
entry---completing in O(1) time without shifting the array.

\subsection{Smart Gossip Integration}
\label{sec:fw_smartgossip}

The forwarding probability is computed by \texttt{gossip\_forward\_probability()},
the firmware implementation of the algorithm described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation} using the compiled parameter values that correspond to
the differential evolution optimum and are embedded as compile-time constants in
\texttt{config.h}, that returns a fixed-point percentage.

Apart from this, the forwarding decision in
\texttt{should\_forward()} draws a uniform random integer from
\texttt{esp\_random()}\,\%\,100 and forwards if it falls below the threshold.
\texttt{esp\_random()} reads the ESP32 hardware random number generator, which is
continuously seeded from RF thermal noise when the BLE radio is
active~\cite{espressif2023esp32trm}, providing statistically independent draws
without the sequential correlations of a software PRNG.

\subsection{Duplicate Detection and Passive ACK Suppression}
\label{sec:fw_passive_ack}

\paragraph{Duplicate detection.}
The duplicate cache stores up to \texttt{DUP\_CACHE\_SIZE}~$= 50$ entries, each
keyed by the (\texttt{pkt\_id},~\texttt{src}) pair and carrying a copy count $c$ and
a reception timestamp; entries expire after \texttt{DUP\_TIMEOUT}~$= 30$\,s, and
once the cache is full, a new entry evicts the least-recently-used one. Since the
copy count fed into $f_{\mathrm{dupe}}$ is read before the forwarding probability is
evaluated, that probability decays monotonically as additional relay paths deliver
copies of the same message, naturally throttling rebroadcast in well-connected
regions.

\paragraph{Passive ACK suppression.}
When the state machine transitions to \texttt{k\_rx\_forward}, it calls
\texttt{mesh\_register\_pending\_forward()} to insert a \texttt{PendingForward}
record---holding the packet identifier and an \texttt{active} boolean initialised to
\texttt{true}---into a mutex-protected table of up to
\texttt{MAX\_PENDING\_FORWARDS}~$= 8$ entries, before posting the
\texttt{tx\_LoRa\_msg\_t} descriptor to \texttt{xLoRaTxQueue}. If, during the jitter
delay, the same message arrives again from a neighbour that forwarded it
independently, the validate state calls \texttt{cancel\_pending\_forward()},
clearing \texttt{active}; when \texttt{vLoRaTxTask} later calls
\texttt{mesh\_consume\_pending\_forward()}, it then finds the record inactive and
discards the descriptor without transmitting---eliminating a redundant on-air copy
with no explicit coordination between nodes as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fw_passive_ack}.

\img{images/passive_ack_sequence_diagram.png}{Passive ACK suppression sequence-diagram.}{0.85}{fw_passive_ack}

\subsection{TTL and Loop Prevention}
\label{sec:fw_ttl}

Network loops are controlled by two independent mechanisms. At the packet level,
every forwarding node decrements the TTL field by one, and once it reaches zero the
frame is discarded at \texttt{k\_rx\_validate} before any duplicate-cache update;
with \texttt{MAX\_TTL}~$= 5$, this bounds the propagation diameter to five hops per
transmission event.

At the message level, the duplicate cache provides a complementary bound: even if a
packet re-enters a previously visited node through an unexpected cycle, the cache
increments its copy count, and once that count exceeds
\texttt{GOSSIP\_MAX\_COPIES}~$= 3$, the packet is dropped unconditionally. Together,
the two limits bound the worst-case replication factor of any message to at most
$3 \times 5 = 15$ on-air copies within its 30-second cache lifetime.


\section{System Services Layer}
\label{sec:fw_services}

\subsection{Power Management and Battery Monitoring}
\label{sec:fw_power}

The A3MESH node operates as a continuously active relay; no deep-sleep or light-sleep
mode is implemented, as passive ACK timers and neighbour-table state must remain
consistent across forwarding events. Transmit energy is bounded instead by the
duty-cycle enforcement mechanism in Section~\ref{sec:fw_lora_driver}, which limits
on-air activity to at most 36\,s per hour without imposing any reception blackout.

Battery state is estimated by the \texttt{Battery} class from a 20-point look-up
table of empirically characterised (voltage, percentage) pairs, ranging from
4.20\,V at 100\,\% down to 3.00\,V at~0\,\%. The input voltage is obtained by
reading the 12-bit ADC on GPIO36 (pin~A0) and scaling by the hardware divider factor
$k = 2.0$, derived from the R13/R14 resistive divider described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware}. Battery percentage and the RSSI of the last received frame
are reported to the companion application via the \texttt{AT+INFO?} command. The
DW01A\,+\,FS8205A protection stage provides a hard cut-off near 2.4\,V as a secondary
safeguard independent of firmware state.

\subsection{Node Identity}
\label{sec:fw_identity}

Each node derives its 32-bit identifier deterministically from the factory-programmed
six-byte Bluetooth MAC address using the FNV-1a non-cryptographic hash
function~\cite{eastlake2011fnv}, computed iteratively as
$h_0 = \mathrm{FNV\_OFFSET\_BASIS}$ and, for $i = 0, \ldots, 5$:
\[
    h_{i+1} = \bigl(h_i \oplus \mathrm{MAC}[i]\bigr) \times \mathrm{FNV\_PRIME}
    \pmod{2^{32}}
\]
with $\mathrm{ID} = h_6$. Since all six MAC bytes are folded into the accumulator
before the final multiplication, the 32-bit output is sensitive to the full MAC
address while remaining negligibly cheap to compute at boot time, and because it
stays invariant across firmware updates and power cycles, peer devices and the
duplicate cache can maintain consistent state without manual reconfiguration.

\subsection{LoRa-to-BLE Pipeline}
\label{sec:fw_lora2ble}

The downstream pipeline carries received mesh traffic to the smartphone
(Figure~\ref{fig:fw_lora2ble}). A frame arriving over the air triggers the DIO0 ISR,
which wakes \texttt{vLoRaRxTask}; the task reads the frame into a pool slot and posts
the pointer to \texttt{xLoRaRxQueue}. \texttt{LoRa2BLETask} then dequeues the pointer on
Core~1 and drives the reception state machine described in
Section~\ref{sec:fw_statemachine}. If the destination matches the local node (or the
packet is broadcast), the machine reaches \texttt{k\_rx\_local\_delivery}: the formatted
AT notification string is placed on \texttt{xBLETxQueue}, and \texttt{vBLETxTask}
delivers it to the smartphone via a GATT notification. If the packet must instead be
relayed, the machine reaches \texttt{k\_rx\_forward}: a \texttt{tx\_LoRa\_msg\_t}
descriptor is posted to \texttt{xLoRaTxQueue}, re-entering the transmit path on
Core~0. These two transitions are independent rather than mutually exclusive: a
broadcast packet with an affirmative gossip decision triggers both local delivery and a
forwarded copy in the same pass through the state machine.

\section{Security Architecture}
\label{sec:fw_security}

\subsection{Encryption Model}
\label{sec:fw_crypto}

All encrypted payloads use AES-256-GCM as specified in NIST SP~800-38D~\cite{nist2007gcm}
and implemented through the mbedTLS library (\texttt{mbedtls\_gcm\_*} functions),
which on the ESP32 maps to the hardware AES
accelerator~\cite{espressif2023esp32trm}. AES-256-GCM is an authenticated encryption
with associated data (AEAD) scheme~\cite{rfc5116} providing confidentiality and
message authentication in a single pass, without a separate HMAC step. Keys are
256\,bits (32\,bytes); nonces are 96\,bits (12\,bytes); authentication tags are
128\,bits (16\,bytes). Decryption that produces a tag mismatch returns a
\texttt{CryptoError::AUTH\_FAIL} and the plaintext is discarded.

Three encryption scopes are supported. \textbf{Node-to-node encryption}
(\texttt{k\_enc\_node2node}) uses a symmetric key pre-shared between a specific pair
of nodes, providing end-to-end confidentiality for unicast conversations.
\textbf{Group encryption} (\texttt{k\_enc\_group}) uses a single key distributed
to a cohort of nodes, enabling private multicast without sending separate unicasts
per recipient. \textbf{Network encryption} (\texttt{k\_enc\_network}) uses a
mesh-wide shared key as a baseline confidentiality layer against passive
radio observers. Unencrypted messaging (\texttt{k\_data\_plain}) is preserved to
ensure interoperability when key provisioning has not been completed.

\subsection{Nonce Construction and Replay Protection}
\label{sec:fw_nonce}

Nonce uniqueness is guaranteed by the \texttt{NonceManager} class. Each 96-bit nonce
is composed of the 32-bit sender node ID concatenated with a 64-bit monotonically
increasing counter, as recommended in RFC~5116~\cite{rfc5116}:
\[
    \text{nonce}_{96} =
    \bigl[\,\text{node\_id}_{32}\;\|\;\text{counter}_{64}\,\bigr]
\]
The node-ID prefix ensures that two senders sharing a group or network key can never
produce the same nonce even if their per-sender counters momentarily coincide. The
64-bit counter is transmitted in-band as part of the encrypted payload, allowing the
receiver to reconstruct the exact nonce without an additional header field. The
counter is stored as a \texttt{std::atomic<uint64\_t>} and incremented atomically
before each encryption call.

Replay attacks are prevented by the \texttt{ReplayProtection} class, which maintains
per-sender state for up to 16 simultaneous senders. For each sender, it tracks the
highest counter value seen and accepts a new packet only if its counter is strictly
greater than the recorded maximum, or falls within a backward window of 64 positions
to accommodate legitimate out-of-order delivery. Counters falling below the window
floor are unconditionally rejected as replays.

\subsection{Key Management}
\label{sec:fw_keymgmt}

Key material is provisioned through two complementary mechanisms. The AT command
interface allows the companion application to push keys directly over the authenticated
BLE connection (\texttt{AT+SETPEERKEY=}, \texttt{AT+SETGRPKEY=},
\texttt{AT+SETNETKEY=}), storing them in the \texttt{CryptoManager} singleton.
Alternatively, \texttt{AT+SENDKX=\tcmp{dst},\tcmp{key}} constructs a
\texttt{k\_key\_exchange} frame and delivers it over the mesh, enabling one node to
provision a peer key on a remote device without a direct BLE connection to that device;
the recipient's companion application receives a \texttt{+KX=} notification and
confirms the key via \texttt{AT+SETPEERKEY=}. All keys reside in volatile memory only:
\texttt{AT+CLRKEYS} and any power cycle erase the entire key store, requiring
re-provisioning on the next boot.

\chapter{Mobile Application Development}

\section{Application Objectives}

The mobile application is the sole human interface of the A3MESH platform. As established in Section~\ref{sec:design_requirements}, a central requirement of the system is that it must be operable by users who possess no technical background. This requirement places the entire burden of abstraction on the application layer: all radio configuration, mesh routing decisions, duty-cycle enforcement, and cryptographic key management must remain invisible to the end user, who should perceive the system as an ordinary messaging application that happens to function without internet connectivity.

The application targets the Android platform, with API level 21 (Android 5.0) as the minimum supported version and API level 34 as the compilation target. This choice reflects both the dominant market share of Android among the target demographic and the maturity of its Bluetooth Low Energy APIs, which are required to interface with the custom hardware node described in Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware}. iOS support was explicitly excluded from the scope of this work, given time constraints and the fact that the primary research contributions reside in the routing algorithm and the custom hardware platform rather than in the companion application.

Four principal objectives shaped the design of the application. First, it must establish and maintain a reliable BLE connection with the paired A3MESH node, transparently recovering from transient disconnections. Second, it must translate user actions---composing a message, opening a conversation, managing contacts---into the AT commands defined by the firmware interface described in Section~\ref{sec:fw_at}, and dispatch incoming AT notifications to the relevant conversation. Third, it must manage the session cryptographic material required for end-to-end encryption, performing ECDH key exchange and daily network key derivation on behalf of the user without requiring any manual intervention. Fourth, it must persist all conversations and contact entries locally in a structured database so that message history remains accessible regardless of BLE connectivity status.

\section{User Experience Design}

The interaction model adopted for A3MESH deliberately mirrors the conventions of mainstream instant messaging applications, a design decision that flows directly from the accessibility requirement discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:req_accessibility}. Users are presented with a conversation list on launch, from which they can open individual chat threads or initiate a new exchange. Within a thread, messages are rendered as asymmetric chat bubbles---outgoing messages aligned to the right with a filled blue background, and incoming messages aligned to the left with a neutral surface colour---a layout that reproduces the visual grammar established by widely adopted messaging platforms and is immediately recognizable without instruction.

The visual framework follows the Material Design~3 specification~\cite{google2024material3}, which provides a coherent set of colour tokens, typographic scales, and component behaviours optimized for Android. The primary structural element is a floating, pill-shaped bottom navigation bar hosting four destinations: \emph{Chats}, \emph{Contacts}, \emph{Device}, and \emph{Settings}. The pill form is implemented through a custom drawable with 28~dp corner radius, giving the element a visually distinct character consistent with the rounded aesthetic introduced in Material~3. The bar floats over the content layer with a 24~dp bottom margin; all scrollable lists include a matching bottom padding to prevent content from being obscured behind it.

The four-tab architecture maps cleanly to the user's mental model: the Chats tab addresses communication, the Contacts tab manages the address book, the Device tab surfaces connectivity status, and the Settings tab exposes the small subset of radio parameters---RF output power, frequency channel, and local node address---that a user might legitimately wish to adjust. No further configuration is exposed; all other node parameters are set to defaults by the firmware and remain entirely invisible at the application level.

\section{Android Communication Architecture}
\label{sec:app_architecture}

The application follows the Model--View--ViewModel (MVVM) architectural pattern recommended by Google's Android Architecture Guidelines~\cite{google2024architecture}, in which UI components observe reactive data streams rather than pulling state imperatively. This choice was motivated by two properties of the BLE communication domain: incoming events arrive asynchronously on hardware callback threads, not on the Android main thread; and multiple UI components must react to the same events without being coupled to one another.

The central component is \texttt{BleViewModel}, an \texttt{AndroidViewModel} subclass scoped to the \texttt{MainActivity} lifecycle and shared across all fragments via the \texttt{activityViewModels()} delegate. \texttt{BleViewModel} exposes the application's observable state through \texttt{LiveData} objects: the currently connected \texttt{BluetoothDevice}, a human-readable connection status string, an event stream of newly received messages, the node's self-reported device information, and the list of discovered neighbouring nodes. Mutations to this state are posted from whichever thread detects them, and the Android framework guarantees that all observers are notified on the main thread, eliminating the need for explicit thread-marshalling in the UI layer.

Persistent state---messages and contacts---is managed through a Room database, the official Android SQLite abstraction layer~\cite{google2024room}. Two entities are defined: \texttt{Contact}, keyed by the 32-bit integer LoRa node address, and \texttt{Message}, which records the remote address, textual content, timestamp, direction, an optional sender-address field for public-channel messages, and a read/unread flag. Messages are stored in decrypted form; the firmware performs AES-256-GCM decryption before forwarding plaintext over BLE as described in Section~\ref{sec:fw_crypto}, so the application never handles ciphertext directly. Both DAOs return \texttt{LiveData}-wrapped query results, meaning that the conversation list and chat bubbles update reactively whenever the database changes, without any polling.

The BLE connection is maintained across the full Android activity lifecycle through a foreground \texttt{Service}---\texttt{BleConnectionService}---started as soon as a successful GATT connection is established. Android's battery-optimization framework aggressively kills background processes after a period of inactivity; promoting the process to foreground status prevents this termination, at the cost of displaying a persistent notification that informs the user that a LoRa connection is active. The service carries no business logic; its sole purpose is to hold foreground priority so that the \texttt{ConnectionManager} singleton and \texttt{BleViewModel} continue receiving GATT callbacks when the application is minimized.

\paragraph{Key management.} The application hosts a layered key management subsystem that operates transparently to the user and provisions cryptographic material to the node firmware on demand. Three classes of key are managed. The first is the \emph{network-wide daily key}, a 32-byte AES-256 secret derived deterministically from a fixed network seed and the current calendar date using HKDF-SHA256~\cite{rfc5869}:
\begin{equation}
K_{\text{net}} = \text{HKDF-SHA256}\!\left(\text{seed},\,\varnothing,\,\texttt{"A3MESH-NET-DAILY-v1"} \;\|\; \text{date},\,32\right).
\end{equation}
Because the derivation is deterministic and the seed is embedded in every node's firmware, all nodes in the network share the same daily key without any out-of-band coordination; the key rotates automatically at midnight, a transition scheduled by \texttt{BleViewModel} via a coroutine that sleeps until the next midnight boundary.

The second class is \emph{peer-to-peer session keys}, one per remote node, derived from an ECDH handshake over the NIST P-256 curve (secp256r1)~\cite{nist2018sp800_56a}. The handshake is initiated automatically whenever the application sends a first message to a node for which no session key exists, or when the firmware notifies the application of a newly discovered neighbour via a \texttt{+DISC=} unsolicited response. Both parties generate ephemeral P-256 key pairs, exchange the 64-byte raw public keys (X and Y coordinates concatenated, without DER encoding overhead) over the mesh via \texttt{AT+SENDKX} commands, and independently derive the session key as:
\begin{equation}
K_{\text{peer}} = \text{HKDF-SHA256}\!\left(\text{ECDH shared secret},\,\varnothing,\,\texttt{"A3MESH-N2N-v1"},\,32\right).
\end{equation}
The resulting key is provisioned to the firmware via \texttt{AT+SETPEERKEY}, after which all subsequent messages to that peer are encrypted at the firmware level before transmission. The third class is \emph{group keys}, 32-byte secrets generated by a group creator using a cryptographically secure random number generator and distributed out-of-band via a QR code. All keys are persisted in hexadecimal form through the \texttt{A3MeshKeyStore} class, which wraps \texttt{SharedPreferences}, and the full key set is re-provisioned to the firmware on every reconnection to survive firmware resets.

\section{BLE Integration}

The BLE communication layer is encapsulated in the \texttt{ConnectionManager} singleton, which owns the GATT connection lifecycle and serializes all operations through a \texttt{ConcurrentLinkedQueue}. This serialization is necessary because the Android BLE stack is not reentrant: issuing a second GATT operation before the callback for the previous one has fired results in silent failure. The queue ensures that only one operation---connect, read, write, MTU negotiation, or notification subscription---is in flight at any given time, and that the next operation is dequeued only after \texttt{signalEndOfOperation()} is called from the relevant GATT callback. Consumers register \texttt{ConnectionEventListener} instances, stored via \texttt{WeakReference} to avoid leaking Activity contexts, and receive callbacks for connection completion, disconnection, and characteristic changes.

Upon successful GATT connection, \texttt{MainActivity} requests a maximum transmission unit (MTU) of 256 bytes to accommodate AT commands whose payloads may approach the BLE PDU size, then subscribes to notifications on the Nordic UART Service (NUS) RX characteristic~\cite{nordic2023nus} (\texttt{UUID 6E400003}), which the firmware uses to push unsolicited responses. Commands are written to the NUS TX characteristic (\texttt{UUID 6E400002}) as UTF-8-encoded byte arrays terminated with \texttt{\textbackslash r\textbackslash n}, following the ITU-T V.250 convention adopted by the firmware command parser~\cite{itu1999v250}.

Incoming notifications are dispatched in \texttt{MainActivity}'s \texttt{onCharacteristic\-Changed} callback according to a set of unsolicited response codes defined by the firmware AT interface: \texttt{+RCV=} for plain messages, \texttt{+RCVE2E=} for end-to-end encrypted messages, \texttt{+RCVGRP=} for group messages, \texttt{+KX=} for key-exchange payloads, \texttt{+DISC=} for newly discovered neighbour notifications, and \texttt{+NEIGHBORS=} for the bulk neighbour table response. Each code triggers the corresponding handler in \texttt{BleViewModel}, which updates the Room database, posts the appropriate \texttt{LiveData} event, or initiates a key exchange, depending on the type of notification.

\section{Messaging Interface}

The messaging interface is organized around two principal screens. \texttt{ChatsFragment} presents the conversation list as a \texttt{RecyclerView} powered by \texttt{ChatsAdapter}, which uses \texttt{DiffUtil} to compute item differences and animate insertions without redrawing the entire list. Each row displays the contact name or hexadecimal node address, the timestamp of the most recent message, and an unread message badge. A pinned entry at the top of the list represents the public broadcast channel, which is permanently visible. New conversations are initiated through a speed-dial floating action button that expands into three options: direct message to a specific node address, create a new group, and join an existing group.

\texttt{ChatActivity} hosts the active chat window. When the user submits a message, \texttt{BleViewModel.sendMessage()} inspects the key store and selects the appropriate AT command: \texttt{AT+SENDNET} for the public channel, \texttt{AT+SENDGRP} for a group conversation with a provisioned group key, \texttt{AT+SENDE2E} for a peer with an established session key, and \texttt{AT+SEND} for an unkeyed initial exchange, simultaneously triggering a background key exchange to establish encryption for subsequent messages. Outgoing messages are written to the Room database immediately and displayed in the bubble view, providing perceived instant feedback regardless of mesh propagation delay.

Group management relies on QR-code-based key distribution implemented with the ZXing library~\cite{zxing2024}. When a user creates a group, the application generates a random 32-byte AES-256 group key, encodes it together with the 32-bit group identifier and group name into a compact string of the form \texttt{a3g:<groupId>:<keyHex>:<name>}, and renders this string as a QR code for distribution. Members wishing to join scan the code using ZXing's \texttt{ScanContract} Activity Result API, whereupon the application extracts the group key, provisions it to the firmware via \texttt{AT+SETGRPKEY}, and opens the corresponding group chat.

\section{Contact and Node Management}

The Contacts subsystem bridges two abstractions: the 32-bit integer LoRa node addresses used by the firmware and the human-readable names with which users identify their peers. \texttt{ContactsFragment} allows the user to assign a name to any node address, modify existing entries, and delete contacts. Contacts are resolved against message records at display time; if no contact entry exists for a given address, the application falls back to the raw hexadecimal representation (e.g., \texttt{0xAABBCCDD}).

\texttt{DeviceFragment} provides visibility into the paired node's operational state. On connection, the application queries the node by issuing \texttt{AT+INFO?}, and the response---containing the node identifier, firmware version, battery level, radio channel, spreading factor, bandwidth, and RF output power---is parsed and displayed. A separate \texttt{AT+NEIGHBORS?} query retrieves the current neighbour table, listing the identifiers, received signal strength, and last-heard timestamps of all nodes within direct radio range of the paired hardware. This list is also used proactively: on connection establishment and on each \texttt{+DISC=} notification, the application automatically initiates ECDH key exchange with newly discovered peers, so that end-to-end encrypted communication is available as soon as a neighbour enters range, without any user action.

\section{Offline Interaction Model}

A deliberate consequence of the architectural choices described above is that the application remains functional in a meaningful sense even when no A3MESH node is paired. The Room database retains the full conversation history and contact book, which are accessible and browsable at all times. Attempting to send a message with no BLE device connected is detected at the \texttt{ConnectionManager} level and results in a Material alert dialog prompting the user to establish a connection before proceeding. This clear feedback, rather than silent failure, is consistent with the accessible design philosophy that underpins the platform.

The foreground service ensures that BLE connectivity persists when the application moves to the background---a practical requirement in emergency scenarios where the device may be pocketed while the user is engaged in another activity. Should the BLE link be interrupted, the application displays an alert dialog on next resumption and the foreground service terminates itself cleanly.

\section{Development Strategy}

\subsection{Rapid Development Using Claude Code}

The mobile application was developed with the assistance of Claude Code, an AI-based programming tool produced by Anthropic, which served as an interactive development partner throughout the implementation phase. AI programming assistants of this class, built on large language models trained on extensive code corpora, are capable of generating, refactoring, and debugging code at the function and module level in response to natural-language specifications, significantly compressing the iteration cycle for routine implementation tasks~\cite{dakhel2023copilot}.

In practice, the architectural skeleton of the application---the Room database schema, the ViewModel layer, the Navigation Component graph, and the RecyclerView adapter infrastructure---was generated across a small number of interactive sessions, with the developer acting as architect and reviewer rather than as typist. The cryptographic subsystem required closer collaboration: the key exchange protocol was specified at the algorithmic level and the resulting code was cross-checked line by line against the specifications of RFC~5869 and NIST~SP~800-56A. AI-generated cryptographic code demands particularly careful human review, because errors in key size, encoding convention, or error propagation can be syntactically valid yet semantically incorrect; every generated function in the \texttt{crypto/} package was independently verified before integration.

\subsection{Cost and Time Optimization}

The decision to delegate a substantial fraction of application development to AI-assisted tooling was driven by a resource allocation argument. The mobile application, while essential for the platform's usability, is not the primary research contribution of this thesis. Developing a full-featured Android application involves a significant investment of time in areas---lifecycle management, adapter diffing, theme attribute hierarchies---that are well-understood engineering problems with no research content. Delegating this work to an AI assistant allowed the total time spent on the application to remain proportionate to its role, without sacrificing the functional completeness required for integration testing and real-world validation.

\subsection{Engineering Trade-Offs}

The use of AI-assisted development introduced specific trade-offs that should be acknowledged honestly. The initial code base was derived from an open-source BLE starter project~\cite{punchthroughble2024}, which was progressively transformed into the application described in this chapter. This layered approach occasionally produced architectural inconsistencies; a notable example is that \texttt{ChatActivity} initially used \texttt{by viewModels()} instead of \texttt{by activityViewModels()}, causing the connected-device LiveData to always appear null in that context. Such inconsistencies are characteristic of iterative AI-assisted development and were identified through systematic code review. They do not represent defects in the final delivered application, but they underscore that AI-generated code requires the same level of architectural scrutiny as any other code.

\subsection{Focus Allocation Towards Core Research}

The total development effort invested in the mobile application was approximately two weeks of part-time work, compared with the three months devoted to the simulation framework, the Smart Gossip algorithm, and the custom hardware design. This allocation reflects the primary objectives of the thesis. The application is sufficiently complete for its intended purpose: it enables real-world field testing of the full A3MESH platform, provides an accessible user interface that validates the accessibility requirement of Section~\ref{subsec:req_accessibility}, and demonstrates that the firmware AT interface is expressive enough to support a fully featured messaging application with end-to-end encryption. Future extensions, including iOS support, push notification integration, and message acknowledgement indicators, are deferred to future work.

\section{Application Testing}

Testing of the mobile application was conducted at two levels. Unit tests covering the cryptographic utility classes---\texttt{EcdhHelper}, \texttt{HkdfSha256}, and \texttt{NetworkKeyManager}---were implemented using the standard JVM test runner and verified that key generation, ECDH agreement, and HKDF derivation produce outputs of the correct length and format. The Room data access layer was verified against an in-memory database instance, confirming that message insertion, contact resolution, and unread count aggregation produce the correct results under insertion and deletion.

End-to-end integration testing was performed using two physical A3MESH nodes and two Android devices. A message sent from one device was observed to arrive at the second device's conversation thread, with all intermediate steps---AT command encoding, BLE write, mesh forwarding via Smart Gossip, GATT notification, response parsing, database insertion, and LiveData propagation---confirmed through logcat output. Encrypted communication was validated by verifying that a message sent after ECDH key exchange arrived tagged as \texttt{+RCVE2E=} rather than the plain \texttt{+RCV=} format, and that its decrypted content was correctly rendered in the chat view. Group messaging was verified by creating a group on one device, sharing the generated QR code, scanning it on a second device, and confirming bidirectional message delivery within the group channel.

\chapter{Development Methodology and Project Management}
\label{ch:methodology}

\section{Development Workflow}

The development of A3MESH was structured around a three-layer management model that connected the highest level of planning to the day-to-day engineering workflow. At the top level, Scrum sprints provided the iteration cadence and the mechanism for regular alignment with the project supervisor. At the intermediate level, Jira served as the task-tracking tool in which each sprint's work items were formalized, assigned a unique ticket identifier, and tracked to completion. At the lowest level, a Git branching strategy tied each task identifier to a specific version-control branch, creating unambiguous traceability between project planning and repository history. The interaction between these three tools---Scrum cadence, Jira tickets, and Git branches---is described in the sections that follow.

\section{Agile Methodology}

A lightweight variant of the Scrum framework~\cite{schwaber2020scrum} was adopted for the duration of the project. Sprints of two weeks each provided the iteration unit. At the opening of each sprint, a planning meeting between the student and the project supervisor was held to review the work completed in the preceding iteration, identify any deviations from the original plan, and select the tasks to be addressed in the forthcoming two weeks. At the close of each sprint, a brief review meeting served to demonstrate the increment produced---whether a functioning firmware feature, a set of simulation results, or a drafted thesis chapter---and to gather feedback before proceeding further.

The two-week cadence was chosen as a balance between feedback frequency and iteration overhead. Shorter cycles would have imposed disproportionate planning cost in a project where some tasks---such as PCB fabrication or simulation campaigns---have an inherent minimum duration of several days. Longer cycles would have risked accumulating divergence from the supervisor's expectations before a correction could be applied. In practice, the sprint boundary also served as a forcing function for integration: at the end of each sprint, the increment was required to be testable on physical hardware or demonstrable in a running application, which prevented the gradual accumulation of partially finished work.

When a technical challenge proved larger than anticipated within a sprint---as occurred, for instance, during the implementation of the AES-256-GCM encryption pipeline in the firmware, which required a full sprint extension---the scope of the subsequent sprint was adjusted to compensate, rather than deferring indefinitely. This continuous re-prioritization is precisely the mechanism that Scrum is designed to support and that distinguishes it from a rigid waterfall plan.

\section{Sprint Planning with Jira}

Each sprint's work items were recorded in a Jira project board maintained throughout the development period. Every discrete deliverable---a subsystem design, a firmware module, a simulation experiment, a thesis chapter---was represented as a Jira issue assigned a unique ticket identifier of the form \texttt{TFG-\textit{N}}, where $N$ is a monotonically increasing integer. The ticket captured the acceptance criteria for the item, the sprint to which it was assigned, and the time estimate.

To prevent the backlog from becoming a flat, undifferentiated list as the project grew, tickets were grouped under six parent epics that aligned with the principal concerns of the project. The \emph{Documentation} epic collected all tasks related to the drafting of thesis chapters and the recording of design notes for later reference. The \emph{Debugging and Testing} epic covered real-world validation activities, including field tests and hardware bring-up sessions. The \emph{FW Architecture} epic tracked the implementation of all firmware modules described in Chapter~\ref{ch:firmware}. The \emph{HW Architecture} epic grouped the KiCad schematic design, PCB layout, and manufacturing tasks of Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware}. The \emph{System Architecture} epic covered the network simulation framework and the Smart Gossip algorithm development of Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation}. Finally, the \emph{Mobile SW} epic contained all tasks associated with the Android application described in Chapter~\ref{ch:mobileapp}. This two-level hierarchy---epics grouping individual tickets---made it possible to assess progress at a subsystem granularity during planning meetings without losing sight of the individual task level.

This ticket-centric representation served two purposes beyond simple task tracking. First, it made the scope of each sprint explicit and negotiable at the planning meeting, preventing scope creep from being introduced informally between meetings. Second, and more importantly for day-to-day engineering, the ticket identifier was embedded directly in the version-control branch name, as described in the following section, creating a direct and auditable link between a Jira ticket and all the commits that contributed to resolving it.

\section{Version Control with Git}

The project used Git~\cite{chacon2014progit} for version control throughout its entire duration. The repository's branching strategy was designed around a single invariant: the \texttt{main} branch must always represent a compilable, functional state of the project. No commit was ever made directly to \texttt{main}; all development work was carried out on dedicated feature branches.

Feature branches follow the naming convention \texttt{feature\_TFG-\textit{N}\_\textit{summary}}, where \textit{N} is the Jira ticket number and \textit{summary} is a concise, underscore-separated description of the task. For example, the branch on which this chapter was drafted is \texttt{feature\_TFG-52\_Methodology}, and the branch that produced the hardware chapter was \texttt{feature\_TFG-48\_Hardware}. The inclusion of the Jira ticket number in the branch name means that any line of the repository's \texttt{git log} can be traced back to the originating task without consulting external documentation.

The integration workflow follows a deliberate sequence. When starting a new task, a feature branch is created from the current tip of \texttt{main}. All development commits are placed on that branch. Before integrating the completed work, the latest state of \texttt{main} is pulled into the feature branch to incorporate any changes that may have been merged by other branches during the development period, resolving any conflicts at this stage so that \texttt{main} itself is never the site of a conflict resolution. The developer then checks out \texttt{main} and merges the feature branch into it:

\begin{verbatim}
git checkout main
git merge feature_TFG-N_summary
\end{verbatim}

Figure~\ref{fig:git_workflow} illustrates this sequence as a branch diagram, showing the moment at which \texttt{main} is pulled into the feature branch before the final merge, thereby ensuring compatibility with any concurrent work that has already been integrated.

If the merge introduces a regression, it is detectable immediately by building the project on \texttt{main} before the branch is pushed to the remote, and can be corrected or reverted without the history of \texttt{main} ever containing a broken commit visible to a collaborator. The feature branch is retained after the merge so that its history remains accessible for reference.

\section{Repository Organization}

The entire project is maintained within a single repository whose directory structure enforces the orthogonality of its constituent subsystems. The root of the repository contains the LaTeX source of this thesis alongside four subdirectories, each of which corresponds to one self-contained technical component: \texttt{tfg\_firmware/} holds the ESP-IDF firmware project, \texttt{tfg\_hardware/} holds the KiCad PCB schematic and layout files, \texttt{tfg\_software/} holds the Android application Gradle project, and \texttt{MeshtasticatorMeshAlgoComp/} holds the modified Meshtasticator simulation framework.

This monorepo organization was chosen deliberately so that a complete snapshot of the project at any point in history---including the thesis text, the firmware, the hardware design, and the simulation code---is recoverable from a single commit hash. The orthogonality of the subdirectories ensures that changes to one subsystem do not generate noise in the diffs of another: a firmware commit touches only files under \texttt{tfg\_firmware/}, a thesis chapter commit touches only \texttt{tfe.tex} and \texttt{tfe.bib}, and the two are never conflated. This discipline also makes it straightforward to understand the scope of any given feature branch from its commit history alone.

\section{Continuous Testing Strategy}

Rather than reserving testing for a dedicated phase at the end of the project, validation was integrated into each sprint at the subsystem level. Simulation code was validated by verifying that the Smart Gossip algorithm produced the expected forwarding probabilities for known input values and by running abbreviated simulation campaigns after any change to the routing logic, before committing the results to the feature branch. Firmware modules were tested on physical hardware at each sprint boundary: the firmware was flashed to a pair of ESP32 nodes and the relevant AT commands were exercised manually, with logcat output compared against expected responses. Mobile application code was validated through the combination of JVM unit tests for the cryptographic utility classes and end-to-end integration tests using a physical BLE connection and a live mesh, as described in Section~\ref{ch:mobileapp}.

The sprint-aligned testing discipline meant that integration failures were discovered within days of being introduced rather than at the end of the project, when resolving them would have been substantially more costly. The most significant integration issue encountered---a mismatch between the AES-256-GCM nonce format expected by the firmware decryption routine and the nonce provisioned by the mobile application---was identified and corrected within the sprint in which the encryption pipeline was implemented, rather than surfacing during final validation.

\section{Documentation Practices}

The primary documentation artifact of the project is this thesis, whose chapters were drafted in parallel with the corresponding implementation sprints rather than retrospectively. Writing a chapter concurrently with its associated implementation serves a dual purpose: it forces the author to articulate design decisions clearly at the time they are made, while the rationale is fresh, and it distributes the writing workload evenly across the project timeline rather than concentrating it at the end.

Each subsystem directory additionally contains a \texttt{CLAUDE.md} file that documents the architecture, conventions, and build commands for that component. These files were maintained as developer-facing reference documentation for use during AI-assisted development sessions, as discussed in Section~\ref{ch:mobileapp}, and incidentally serve as concise technical summaries of each subsystem's design decisions.
